### Listen to a Talk in a marine biology class – Questions 25-28

audio: listening-talk1.m4a
image: 19.png

Professor: Let's talk a little about sea otters and their importance to their own and indeed other ecosystems. We'll start with a few basic facts. The sea otter is a marine mammal often found along the coastal waters of the northern Pacific. It lacks the blubber of other marine mammals, like whales or seals. But it stays warm in icy waters thanks to its fur, which is the densest of any animal's. It also has unusually large lungs, two and a half times larger than those of similarly sized land mammals, and this helps it stay buoyant and dive efficiently. Sea otters have a loose flap of skin under each forearm that functions like a pocket, which they use for storing food and tools. That's right, tools. Sea otters are one of the few animals that use tools, using clam shells for digging and rocks to open clam shells. Now, wonderful as they may be, why care about sea otters? Because sea otters eat sea urchins, and sea urchins eat kelp. Kelp is a large seaweed that can grow up to thirty meters tall, providing food and shelter for a range of marine life. Kelp forests support a greater variety of marine life than almost any other ocean habitat. Kelp also grows very densely and acts as a natural buffer, protecting shorelines from storm damage. Finally, kelp forests are like rain forests in that they absorb and store carbon dioxide through photosynthesis, mitigating climate change and reducing ocean acidification.

### Listen to a Talk in a literature class – Questions 29-32

audio: listening-talk2.m4a
image: 20.png

Professor: So today we're looking at the hero's journey, a storytelling pattern that shows up in myths, literature, and narratives from all over the world. This structure was brought into the spotlight by Joseph Campbell, an American mythologist. In his book, The Hero with a Thousand Faces, Campbell studied myths across cultures and noticed they shared a common structure. Campbell called it the monomyth, and he believed it reflected something universal about the human experience. The journey usually kicks off with a call to adventure, some kind of invitation or challenge that pulls the hero out of their everyday life. They might resist at first, but eventually they accept, often with a mentor's help. Then come the trials, tests that push the hero to grow. At the center of it all is the abyss, the toughest part, where the hero faces their greatest fear or enemy. If they make it through, they're transformed. And here's the key part. They return with a gift, some kind of insight, wisdom, or ability that can help others. That gift is what makes the journey meaningful, not just for the hero, but for their community. Now, some critics argue this model oversimplifies storytelling. It can flatten out cultural differences and force diverse stories into a single mold. Still, I'd argue it's influential because it taps into something deeply human: the idea that struggle leads to growth, and that we come back changed.

### Listen to a Talk in an archaeology class – Questions 12-15

audio: listening-m2talk2.m4a
image: 27.png

Professor: Often when we think of mummies, we think of ancient Egyptians and their elaborate methods of preserving bodies. We used to think that Egyptians were the only people practicing mummification during the Bronze Age, about four thousand years ago, because typical mummies are found in places with very dry climates, like Egypt. But then archaeologists discovered a site in Scotland with evidence of mummification. And Scotland is, well, wet, rainy. This site is called Cladh Hallan, and it's the only place in Britain where we've found prehistoric Bronze Age mummies. So researchers unearthed skeletons at Cladh Hallan, including a man and a woman who lived around fifteen hundred B.C.E. Since these were just skeletons, the researchers didn't realize at first that they had actually been mummies. That realization came after doing a test called radiocarbon dating on the bones. And radiocarbon dating produced a surprising result. The bones that made up each skeleton had not all come from the same person. In fact, the remains were composites: bones from different individuals, who had died at different times, had been deliberately combined to form a single skeleton. In one case, a pile of bones turned out to belong to two different people. Now, normally, if a body were left to decay for a long period of time, it would turn into a pile of loose bones, not an intact skeleton like the ones that were found. So the bodies must have been purposely preserved for a long time before burial. Why? Well, who knows. But we do know how. The bodies were preserved, mummified, in a peat bog. Peat bogs are kind of like swamps, except their conditions prevent organic materials, like bodies, from fully breaking down. They stay more intact. Prehistoric people in Scotland used to preserve animal fat, like butter, in peat bogs. So they definitely had the concept that bogs could keep things from rotting. It seems they may have applied that same idea to human bodies, and even combined the remains of different people as part of some ritual. Whatever the reason, the discovery forced researchers to rethink the long held assumption that mummification in the Bronze Age was unique to Egypt.
