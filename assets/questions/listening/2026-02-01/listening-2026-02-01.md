image: 19.png
Professor: One of the most fascinating aspects of human culture is gift-giving. While it might seem simple, it's actually a deeply social act shaped by cultural norms. Anthropologists have found that gifts aren't just about exchanging goods, they're about building relationships. In many Indigenous communities, gift-giving plays a central role in ceremonies and rituals. For example, in the potlatch tradition among Indigenous groups in the Pacific Northwest, gifts like food, blankets, or tools are exchanged during communal gatherings to honor guests, mark life events, and reinforce social bonds. These gifts carry meaning and create mutual obligations, helping maintain harmony and unity within the group. Interestingly, receiving a gift often comes with an expectation to give back, not immediately, but eventually. Anthropologist Marcel Mauss described this as a spirit of reciprocity. And what's important is that it's not just a one-time transaction of "you gave me something, so I'll give you something." This is often repeated in a continuous loop, creating long-term connections and trust. Over time, this rhythm of giving and returning strengthens relationships and community ties. Okay, now in more formal settings like business and diplomacy, gift-giving can signal goodwill, but cultural sensitivity is key. For instance, while a potted plant might seem thoughtful and eco-friendly, in some cultures, it can symbolize something negative, making it unsuitable for formal occasions. So knowing what's appropriate really matters.

audio: listening-talk2.m4a
image: 20.png
Professor: I want to tell you about an unusual phenomenon that scientists noticed in the Pacific Ocean in the early 1990s. The scientists were recording sounds using hydrophones positioned on the ocean floor in the North Pacific. Hydrophones are specially designed microphones that allow scientists to collect acoustic data underwater. While most of the undersea noises picked up by the hydrophones emanated from whales, ships' engines, or earthquakes, the scientists monitoring the hydrophones detected a number of sounds that they could not identify. One was a regular series of rising infrasonic tones, that is, tones with a very short wavelength, below those that humans can hear. Initially, these sounds were thought to be the songs of whales migrating in the Pacific. However, scientists who studied whale acoustics eventually ruled out this possibility, and the origin of the upsweep sounds remained unknown. So what is the source of the rising tones detected by the hydrophones? The sounds were ultimately tracked to an area of inferred volcanic seismic activity, a chain of undersea mountains in the South Pacific. Some scientists believe that if these mountains contain an active volcano, the source for the mysterious sounds may be hot volcanic lava coming into contact with cold sea water.

### Listen to a Talk in a Biology Class – Questions 8-11

audio: listening-talk3.m4a
image: 26.png
Professor: Human activities generate large amounts of organic waste, like food scraps, yard waste, and paper products. This waste can build up in landfills, but there it decomposes anaerobically, meaning without oxygen. Anaerobic decomposition results in the release of methane, which significantly contributes to global warming. But we have an alternative to landfills: composting. In composting, organic waste is broken down under special conditions by microorganisms, fungi, and sometimes insects. These organisms break down organic material into a dark, crumbly, earthy-smelling substance called humus. Unlike decomposition in landfills, composting occurs aerobically, with oxygen, and therefore doesn't produce methane gas. The compost pile needs a good balance of four things. One is nitrogen-rich material, also known as greens, like fruit and vegetable scraps. The other is carbon-rich material, or browns, like dry leaves, wood chips, and paper waste. And you also need water and air. Humus, the primary product of composting, is full of essential nutrients, meaning it enriches soil, enhances plant growth, and increases agricultural yields. This reduces the need for chemical fertilizers. Humus-enriched soil also supports beneficial microorganisms, insects, and worms that in turn help decompose organic material and maintain soil health. This creates a virtuous circle. In contrast, chemical fertilizers can boost agricultural productivity in the short term, but they are harmful to these beneficial organisms. They can actually damage the soil in the long run by killing the helpful organisms living in it.

### Listen to a Talk in a Biology Class – Questions 12-15

audio: listening-talk4.m4a
image: 27.png
Professor: Modern fish farming relies on massive amounts of commercial feed to boost fish production. This can cause nutrient overload, where excessive amounts of uneaten food plus waste products trigger algal blooms. Algal blooms are rapid increases in algae, tiny aquatic plants that can cloud the water. When those algal blooms die off, they decompose and deplete oxygen from the water, which can suffocate fish and other life. Of course, the main cause of algal blooms is chemical fertilizers in agricultural runoff, like when water from rain or irrigation flows over fields and carries the fertilizers into rivers and coastal waters. But fish farming causes algal blooms too. An intriguing alternative is, or was, the coastal fish ponds of ancient Hawaii called loko i'a. People in Hawaii are reviving them today. These ponds, built along the shore using mainly lava, capture nutrient-rich runoff from nearby fields, naturally fertilizing the ponds. This was before the days of concentrated artificial fertilizers. Instead of causing algal blooms, the nutrients supported modest growth of algae, which fed local plant-eating fish. The ponds used natural tidal flushing to bring in fresh sea water and remove waste, which kept oxygen levels stable. And here's the clever part: the special sluice gates connecting the ponds to the ocean had small holes. These holes allowed juvenile fish to come in from the ocean and fatten up on the food in the pond, but then prevented them from escaping once they matured, so they could be easily harvested.
