### Listen to a Talk in a Business Class – Questions 1-4

subtitle: Dark Stores
audio: listening_form01_q01_q04_lecture_dark_stores.mp3
image: 1.png

Today we're discussing the concept of dark stores. That is, retail centers that resemble warehouses and are usually located near city centers. Unlike traditional storefronts, they are not designed for walk-in customers, but function as fulfillment hubs optimized for rapid online delivery. The rise of dark stores has been fueled by the growing demand for online shopping, especially in urban areas where space is limited and rent is high. The main appeal of dark stores lies in their efficiency. By eliminating the traditional storefront, businesses can reduce overhead costs and pass savings on to consumers through lower prices. Their proximity to dense urban areas also allows for faster delivery times, meeting the expectations of customers who value speed and convenience. For instance, a company might deliver groceries in under an hour, giving it a major competitive advantage over traditional supermarkets. However, dark stores are not without drawbacks. Their expansion can contribute to the decline of traditional retail spaces, creating economic challenges for local businesses. Employment is another concern. While these centers generate jobs and logistics and delivery, they may reduce the number of customer-facing roles available in physical stores. Looking ahead, many experts predict that dark stores will continue to reshape the retail landscape. Some may adopt advanced automation, further reducing the need for human labor and potentially redefining the shopping experience itself. As with other technological shifts, the key challenge will be balancing efficiency with the broader social and economic implications of these changes.

subtitle: Creative Class and Urban Development
audio: listening_form02_q01_q04_lecture_creative_class_and_urban_development.mp3
image: 2.png

Have you ever wondered why certain urban neighborhoods flourish while others stagnate? A fascinating theory, known as the creative class, offers one explanation. Coined by sociologist Richard Florida, this concept suggests that cities with a high concentration of creative professionals, including artists, scientists, and entrepreneurs, tend to experience more significant economic growth and revitalization. The idea is that these individuals drive innovation and attract others like them, creating a vibrant dynamic atmosphere. However, it's important to note that such a transformation isn't without its drawbacks. When these creative hubs thrive economically, it can inadvertently lead to gentrification. That is, it can cause rising property values and living costs. This often displaces long-standing residents who can no longer afford to live in their own neighborhoods. This process can erode the unique cultural fabric that made the area attractive in the first place. Despite this, cities continue to pursue the creative class strategy. They invest in cultural infrastructure, like galleries and performance spaces, to lure creative professionals. Yet, critics argue that this focus on attracting the creative class might overshadow the needs of other community members, particularly those in lower income brackets. It's a delicate balancing act that cities must navigate carefully, fostering economic vitality while ensuring inclusivity and equity. So, next time you stroll through a bustling urban district filled with lively cafes and art installations, consider the multifaceted dynamics at play that underpin its success.

subtitle: Indian Pangolin
audio: listening_form03_q01_q04_lecture_indian_pangolin.mp3
image: 3.png

Imagine an animal so well protected that even a hungry tiger walks away frustrated. Meet the Indian Pangolin, a nocturnal mammal covered in overlapping scales that act like a suit of armor. These scales are made of keratin, the same protein found in human fingernails. They're a powerful defense mechanism. When it encounters an enemy, the Pangolin performs volvation, curling into a tight ball, providing itself with an impenetrable shield. This remarkable use of volvation makes Pangolins exceptional among mammals. Few other creatures can achieve such a complete protective posture. Pangolins also have strong claws for digging and a long sticky tongue that can extend up to 25 centimeters, perfect for capturing ants and termites. They lack teeth, so instead of chewing, they grind their food in their stomach. Their stomach is lined with keratin plates, similar to their outer scales. These plates are extremely hard and work together with the contractions of the stomach to crush and grind captured prey. Now, although the Indian Pangolin's armor deters most predators, tigers, leopards and hyenas occasionally manage to pry them open. Still, the greatest threat to the Indian Pangolin is illegal hunting for scales and meat, which along with habitat destruction have pushed it toward endangered status. Why does this matter? Well, Pangolins are ecological guardians. By consuming millions of insects, they prevent pest outbreaks that could harm crops and forests. Their digging also helps air get into the soil, improving plant health. Losing Pangolins would disrupt these processes, creating ripple effects across various ecosystems.

### Listen to a Talk in a Chemistry Class – Questions 1-4

subtitle: The Accidental Discovery of PTFE
audio: listening_form04_q01_q04_lecture_the_accidental_discovery_of_ptfe.mp3
image: 4.png

Back in 1938, a chemist named Roy Plunkett was working to create a new refrigerant, a new cooling substance. Plunkett was looking for an industrial refrigerant to replace the ones that existed at the time, which were toxic and flammable. Some, for example, could cause fatal poisoning if leaked. He was working with a gas called tetrafluoroethylene, or TFE, a highly flammable refrigerant. Plunkett stored TFE gas in cylinders, but one day he noticed that a cylinder of TFE no longer had gas in it. What was there? Well, powder. A solid. The TFE gas had solidified into this new powdery substance, polytetrafluoroethylene, or PTFE. What made PTFE remarkable was its almost unprecedented stability, its resistance to heat, chemicals, and friction. It reacted with other substances, making it ideal for industrial applications, like parts used in the aerospace industry, also medical devices and electronics. Eventually, PTFE began to be used in consumer products. Ever heard of Teflon, the non-stick coating on frying pans? That is PTFE, and it was used in homes around the world for many years. These days, you may be more likely to use stainless steel or cast iron cookware due to concerns with PTFE. Nevertheless, this is a classic example of serendipity in science. Plunkett wasn't trying to invent a non-stick surface, he stumbled upon it. This kind of discovery isn't rare. Scientists discovered x-rays, penicillin, and many other scientific and medical innovations while trying to do something else.

Trilobites were a group of ancient sea creatures. They're classified as arthropods. They're extinct now. Among modern animals, horseshoe crabs probably resemble trilobites the most. As arthropods, both trilobites and horseshoe crabs have segmented bodies, joints in their legs, and hard exoskeletons, kind of like shells. So we've known for years that trilobites had two compound eyes. There are different kinds of compound eyes, but for many trilobites it meant that their eyes had thousands of tiny lenses. This is helpful for detecting motion, like noticing other creatures swimming around. Other types of compound eyes provided, say sharper images, but having thousands of lenses was most common for trilobites. But in a recent study, researchers examined a trilobite fossil that was damaged. Part of its head was missing. This allowed them to notice, in the middle of the trilobite's forehead, three tiny, dark spots. They said, hmm, these don't seem random. In fact, the tiny spots are evidence of a median eye, that is, a third eye. Most arthropods have median eyes, which aren't really like compound eyes, they're simpler, but a median eye had never been found in trilobites before. Why? Well, researchers had believed it was only there during the larval stage, early in the development of a young trilobite. So researchers thought adult fossils wouldn't have it, but it would have been hidden underneath a thin, dark layer of shell, which was missing in this fossil, so you wouldn't see it in a fossil whose entire head was intact.

Before we say any more about textile production, let's talk about the historical indigo trade. Indigo's a natural blue dye coming from the leaves of indigo fara plants. Today you see it most often in blue jeans. Indigo was a staple export for hundreds of years. There was a time when only rice was more valuable. The English began importing it during the early 1600s from India and British colonists started growing it in North America about a century later. Natural indigo nearly vanished from industrial use by the early 20th century after German chemists invented a synthetic indigo. Of course this was cheaper and more efficient in large scale production. And for decades the natural stuff survived only in niche applications and local traditions. Today indigo is back as a viable commercial crop in places like Bangladesh and the US. This is partly thanks to the fashion industry's efforts to be more environmentally sustainable and many now see natural indigo as a symbol of ethical consumption. Its value as a commodity also lies in its versatility. It's also used in cosmetics and food coloring. Farmers find it to be low maintenance with high returns. One hectare can yield thousands of kilograms of leaves. The economic incentive is clear. Minimal input, high output and growing international interest. That said, the traditional fermentation techniques for extracting the dye are extremely labor intensive, which makes natural indigo expensive. And some producers claiming natural methods still rely on harsh chemicals, which undermines the dye's eco-friendly reputation.
