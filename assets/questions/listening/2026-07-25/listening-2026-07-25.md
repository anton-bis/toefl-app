### Listen to a Talk in an Art History Class - Questions 25-28

subtitle: Stained Glass Art History
audio: listening_m1_q25_q28_lecture_stained_glass_art.mp3
image: 13.png

Professor: Today we are exploring the luminous world of stained glass. When you think of stained glass, you probably think of windows in churches, but it is found outside of churches as well. In fact, it used to brighten many homes across the United States.
Professor: In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, stained glass windows became a beloved feature in domestic architecture thanks to the Arts and Crafts movement. The Arts and Crafts movement celebrated craftsmanship and natural beauty, and stained glass fit the bill perfectly.
Professor: Artists used several techniques to bring their visions to life. The lead method, a traditional approach, involved joining pieces of glass with strips of lead, ideal for bold geometric designs. Then came the copper foil technique popularized by the American artist and designer Louis Comfort Tiffany. This method wrapped each glass piece in thin copper foil, which allowed for more intricate designs like florals and landscapes.
Professor: But these beautiful windows were not without drawbacks. They often lacked proper insulation, leading to heat loss in colder climates. Homeowners had to weigh aesthetics against practicality, sometimes adding storm windows or heavy drapes to compensate. Stained glass windows eventually faded from popularity in domestic architecture, but they are enjoying a quiet renaissance in contemporary art. Modern artists blend old techniques with new technology, like laser cutting, digital design, and fused glass methods, to create contemporary pieces for homes, galleries, and public spaces.

### Listen to a Talk in a Biology Class - Questions 8-11

subtitle: Frog Population Biology
audio: listening_m2_q08_q11_lecture_frog_population.mp3
image: 20.png

Professor: Frogs are not just their croaks and leaps; they are key players in ecosystems, acting as both predators and prey. But scientists have been sounding the alarm. Frog populations are shrinking fast across the globe.
Professor: One major reason is chemical pollution. Pesticides and other pollutants often end up in the water where frogs live and breed. These chemicals interfere with their reproductive systems, leading to fewer eggs and developmental issues in tadpoles. Over time, this can cause entire populations to collapse.
Professor: Another serious threat is disease, especially a fungus called chytrid. This fungus attacks the skin of frogs, which is vital for absorbing water and regulating electrolytes. Infected frogs often become lethargic, lose coordination, and eventually die. Chytrid has spread rapidly and is responsible for declines and extinctions in hundreds of frog species worldwide.
Professor: Frogs also serve as indicators of environmental health. Because they are sensitive to changes in water quality and temperature, their decline often signals deeper ecological problems. Bees play a similar role. When bee populations drop, it can point to issues like pesticide overuse or habitat stress. So when frogs and bees are struggling, it is a warning sign that the environment needs attention.

### Listen to a Talk in an Art History Class - Questions 12-15

subtitle: Cubism Art
audio: listening_m2_q12_q15_lecture_cubism_art.mp3
image: 21.png

Professor: If you have ever looked at a Picasso painting and thought, wait, is that a face or a guitar, you have already encountered the magic of Cubism. This revolutionary movement shook up the art scene in the early twentieth century. It was not just a new style. It was a complete reimagining of how we see and represent reality.
Professor: Artists like Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque broke away from the traditional rules of perspective and instead chose to depict objects from multiple angles at once. Imagine seeing every side of a coffee cup, top, bottom, and side, all in a single image. That is the kind of visual remix Cubism brought to the canvas.
Professor: At first, people did not know what to make of it. Critics called it chaotic, even nonsensical. But over time, Cubism proved to be a game changer, influencing not just painting and sculpture, but also music, literature, and architecture. It encouraged creators to rethink structure, narrative, and form and to reconsider how we think about representation and abstraction. It also paved the way for other art movements like Modernism, which embraced abstraction and conceptual depth in somewhat different ways.
