Professor: Imagine a chilly mountain forest. It's early spring, the snow's just melting, and it's still very cold. Now picture a small, bright yellow flower blooming at the edge of the snow. Hard to imagine any flower surviving in those conditions, right? Yet the snow buttercup thrives. So what's its secret? Well, the snow buttercup's anatomy has a few things going for it. First, a fuzzy stem, like a tiny winter coat, that protects it from extreme cold and wind, plus a deep root system. The roots keep it anchored in unstable soil on mountains and can reach water that's up to thirty centimeters deep. Now, the snow buttercup needs to protect itself from ice crystals. To do this, it produces antifreeze proteins. Researchers are studying these proteins, hoping to develop ways to protect other plants, like maybe developing frost resistant crops for farmers to grow. OK, so these adaptations allow the snow buttercup to bloom earlier, while most other plants are still dormant for winter. This means exclusive visits from beneficial pollinators, like bumblebees and butterflies. The pollinators get food, nectar, from the snow buttercup, while also helping the plant reproduce by spreading its reproductive material, the pollen. The adaptation I find most fascinating is called heliotropic movement. When the flower is open, heliotropic movement means it turns to face the sun throughout the day, staying warm as the sun moves along the sky. Increasing its temperature even a few degrees this way can be a huge advantage when the air is still freezing.

### Listen to a Talk in a psychology class – Questions 29-32

audio: listening-talk2.m4a
image: 20.png

Professor: Positive reinforcement is a powerful concept in psychology and behavior training. It means giving a reward after someone performs an action you want them to do again. The basic idea is that when a behavior leads to a positive outcome, it's more likely to be repeated. In other words, we tend to repeat actions that bring us something positive. This principle applies to both humans and animals, but the way it works can differ. Animals need to receive rewards immediately after the desired behavior. For example, if a dog sits and gets a treat right away, it learns that sitting leads to something good. If the reward comes too late, the connection is lost. Humans, however, can understand delayed rewards. They might work hard today because they're aiming for a future goal, like earning a degree, or they might feel motivated by praise, even if the reward isn't tangible or instant. But rewards must be used carefully. If a parent gives a child candy to stop whining, the child may learn that whining gets rewards. It's like watering weeds instead of flowers. You're encouraging the wrong behavior. And if rewards are given too frequently, they can reduce a person's internal motivation, making them rely more on external incentives to take action. So while positive reinforcement is effective, it works best when applied thoughtfully and with clear intent.

### Listen to a Talk in a business class – Questions 8-11

audio: listening-m2talk1.m4a
image: 26.png

Professor: Today we're discussing the concept of dark stores, retail centers that resemble warehouses and are usually located near city centers. Unlike traditional storefronts, they are not designed for walk-in customers, but function as fulfillment hubs optimized for rapid online delivery. The rise of dark stores has been fueled by the growing demand for online shopping, especially in urban areas where space is limited and rent is high. The main appeal of dark stores lies in their efficiency. By eliminating the traditional storefront, businesses can reduce overhead costs and pass the savings on to consumers at lower prices. Their proximity to dense urban areas also allows for faster delivery times, meeting the expectations of customers who value speed and convenience. For instance, a company might deliver groceries in under an hour, giving it a major competitive advantage over traditional supermarkets. However, dark stores are not without drawbacks. Their expansion can contribute to the decline of traditional retail spaces, creating economic challenges for local businesses. Employment is another concern. While these centers generate jobs in logistics and delivery, they may reduce the number of customer-facing roles available in physical stores. Looking ahead, many experts predict that dark stores will continue to reshape the retail landscape. Some may adopt advanced automation, further reducing the need for human labor and potentially redefining the shopping experience itself. As with other technological shifts, the key challenge will be balancing efficiency with the broader social and economic implications of these changes.

### Listen to a Talk in an archaeology class – Questions 12-15

audio: listening-m2talk2.m4a
image: 27.png

Professor: You might picture archaeologists using tools to dig through dirt and uncover a variety of artifacts, like pottery and jewelry. You might think of a site like Ephesus, an ancient city located in western Turkey. By the way, some say that's the largest excavated site on earth. Ephesus includes massive remains from different time periods, including Roman, Greek, and Byzantine periods, which are different civilizations spanning millennia. For example, at Ephesus, there are ruins of ancient Greek temples and Roman theaters. Other archaeological sites, though, are more difficult to reach. There's an entire subfield of archaeology that focuses on these sites. It's called underwater archaeology. Underwater archaeology is the study of history through sites and artifacts found beneath the water's surface. This includes shipwrecks, submerged settlements, harbors, and ports. Researchers have found all kinds of artifacts at underwater sites, including tools, pottery, coins, and other objects. Because water can slow decay, especially of organic materials like wood or textiles, underwater sites often provide exceptionally well-preserved evidence of past human activity. One notable underwater site is the Uluburun shipwreck off the coast of Turkey, dating to about three thousand years ago. Uluburun was discovered in 1982. Specialized diving and excavation techniques were used at this site, and archaeologists recovered cargo including copper ingots and luxury goods. These artifacts shed light on ancient trade networks. They suggest that highly complex, multi-regional trade routes connecting Central Asia to Europe and Africa existed far earlier than we expected.
