### Listen to a Talk in a Materials Science Class – Questions 5-8

subtitle: Nanotechnology [AI 补全音频]
audio: listening_q05_q08_lecture_nanotechnology_ai.m4a

Professor: Nanoparticles are particles whose size is less than one ten-thousandth of a millimeter. They are far too small to see with an ordinary microscope, yet they are already part of many products we use every day. In this lecture, I want to describe several of their applications. One common example is sunscreen. Older sunscreens left a thick white residue on the skin, but newer sunscreens use titanium dioxide nanoparticles, which block ultraviolet sunlight while remaining transparent. Silver is another useful material at this scale. Silver has antimicrobial properties, and silver nanoparticles can be applied to a wound or a surface to stop bacterial growth. Humans have actually used nanoparticles for millennia, though they did not understand them. Many Roman pink tesserae contain gold nanoparticles, which affect how light interacts with the glass and create the color. Researchers today are also exploring nanoparticles in medicine, where they can carry drugs to specific cells, and in energy, where they can improve the performance of solar cells. Because their properties change so dramatically at this scale, the same material can behave in ways that would be impossible in bulk. As you can see, the same tiny particles can serve very different purposes, and their unusual properties continue to suggest new technologies.

### Listen to a Talk in an Art History Class – Questions 9-12

subtitle: Expressionism
audio: listening_q09_q12_lecture_expressionism.mp3

Professor: Expressionism is an influential modernist art movement that emerged in the early 20th century. This movement is characterized by its focus on representing emotional experience rather than physical reality. Expressionist artists aim to convey their inner feelings and perceptions through distorted and exaggerated forms, vivid colors, and bold brush strokes. One of the most famous expressionist artists is Edvard Munch. His painting, The Scream, shows an agonized person crying out. It eventually became an icon of the movement's focus on intense personal experience. Expressionism was not limited to visual arts, however. It also influenced the literary, theater, and cinematic arts. In literature, authors like Franz Kafka explored themes of alienation and existential angst in his bizarre dream-like fiction. Expressionist theater often used dramatic lighting and intense performances to evoke emotional responses. Sets sometimes featured oversized symbolic objects. In Elmer Rice's 1923 play, The Adding Machine, a giant adding machine represents the dehumanizing forces of technology. Similarly, the German expressionist film movement used innovative techniques, such as cutting between scenes with a shared sound, to help create emotionally charged narratives. Despite its impact, expressionism faced criticism for its departure from traditional aesthetics and its emphasis on the personal. Critics argued that its focus on subjective emotion robbed the works of broader content or meaning, and the bold brushwork in paintings was sometimes viewed as lacking in skill and technique. Nonetheless, expressionism paved the way for later movements such as surrealism, leaving a lasting legacy in the art world.

### Listen to a Talk: Industrial Revolution and Cities – Questions 13-16

subtitle: Industrial Revolution and Cities
audio: listening_q13_q16_lecture_industrial_cities.mp3

Professor: By 1850, the physical layout of an industrial city was completely different from that of its predecessors. How do we picture a medieval city? It's surrounded by walls to protect it from invasion from the neighbouring city. The emergence of nation states did away with this need for protection from invasion, at least on the city scale. And so, over the centuries, the walls were removed and city boundaries pushed out dramatically. Industrial workplaces had been small craft shops, but now they were huge factories located in large factory districts with separate housing for workers constructed nearby. Medieval street layouts that had developed haphazardly were built over, replaced by a gridiron street pattern. Each lot was uniform and predictable, which made it easier to buy and sell property. Many cities kept their central marketplaces, but commercial structures became more prominent in city centres, often more so than cathedrals. Manchester, England, is a good example of this. It grew rapidly during the Industrial Revolution and commercial buildings dominated the city centre. Its layout reflected the new priorities of industrial capitalism.
