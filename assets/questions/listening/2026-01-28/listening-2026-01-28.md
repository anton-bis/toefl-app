Professor: Humans have been expressing themselves through art for thousands of years. Consider ancient cave paintings that have been discovered throughout the world, some of them more than 40,000 years old. Caves in Sulawesi, Indonesia, that include figurative art are an example of this. Some forms of art, though, have much more recent origins. Take, for example, decalcomania, a really cool art technique that originated in the 18th century. So what is decalcomania? At its core, it's a simple process. You begin by spreading paint onto a surface like a sheet of paper. Then you press another surface, let's say canvas, onto the paper. So you press the two surfaces together. At this point, you pull them apart while the paint is still wet. The resulting image is often rich with organic abstract patterns. Decalcomania was first used to decorate pottery in the 18th century, but it really took off in the 20th century, especially with the Surrealist movement. Artists like Max Ernst loved this technique because it helped them tap into the subconscious and explore the unexpected. Ernst was enchanted by the process that allowed him to create shapes that the artist could not fully control. What's especially intriguing about decalcomania is the balance it strikes between control and chaos. Sure, the artist chooses the colors and the materials, but once the process is complete, the final result is mostly up to chance.

audio: listening-part1.m4a
>> play: 13:17 - 14:47
image: 20.png
Professor: Have you ever been so engrossed in a task that you completely missed something obvious? This is called inattentional blindness, a phenomenon where concentrating on one thing can make us overlook something happening right in front of us. A classic study illustrates this perfectly. Participants watched a video of people passing a basketball and were asked to count passes made by players in white shirts. Midway through the video, a person in a gorilla suit walked into the scene, thumped its chest, and left. Astonishingly, about half the viewers never saw the gorilla. Their attention was locked on counting passes. In real-world situations, inattentional blindness can have serious consequences. For example, while driving, focusing on finding a street sign might make you miss a pedestrian stepping into the road. Recognizing this tendency can help us stay safer and more aware. Interestingly, psychologists trace this phenomenon to a useful ability: selective attention. This is the brain's way of filtering sensory input to avoid overload, allowing us to concentrate on important tasks. Think about sitting in a noisy open-plan office writing a report. Phones are ringing, people are talking, but you manage to tune it all out and focus on your work. That's selective attention at its best.

### Listen to a Talk on a psychology podcast – Questions 8-11

audio: listening-part2.m4a
>> play: 1:26 - 3:27
image: 26.png
Professor: There are many different factors that influence human behavior. One is related to social influence, or the idea that our behavior can be shaped by group pressure. To test this idea, a psychologist named Solomon Asch conducted a famous study in the 1950s which has become hugely influential in the field. In his experiment, Asch invited American university students to take part in a simple visual judgment task. Each participant was placed in a group of seven to nine people, all but one of whom were working with the researcher. The group members were shown a standard line on one card and three comparison lines on another card. One comparison line matched the standard while the other two were clearly different lengths. Basically, the correct choice was totally obvious. Each person announced aloud which line matched the standard, and those who were in on the study deliberately gave the wrong answer first. The real participant answered last. Across 12 trials, 75 percent of participants conformed with the incorrect answers that people in the group had given before them at least once, so they chose the line that was clearly wrong. This indicates strong influence of group pressure. This study was replicated in 1980, roughly 30 years after Asch conducted his experiment. The subjects in this experiment were British university students. Interestingly, conformity was much lower with these participants. This suggests that conformity may be influenced by culture and the country people grow up in.

### Listen to a Talk in an environmental science class – Questions 12-15

audio: listening-part2.m4a
>> play: 4:39 - 7:01
image: 27.png
Professor: Some years ago, the government of Tanzania in Africa proposed building a 452-kilometer paved road in the north of the country, with the goal of boosting transportation and economic growth. The proposed route, though, would go through Serengeti National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage site, and conservationists raised alarms. The road would bisect the annual migration corridor of wildebeests and other animals, potentially putting enormous pressure on their populations and disrupting the ecosystem in general. The models used by the conservationists showed that blocking the corridor with the road could reduce wildebeest populations by up to one third, disrupt predator-prey dynamics, and fragment an ecosystem that supports lions, cheetahs, and countless other species. Increased traffic would also heighten poaching risks and introduce invasive species. The evidence wasn't conclusive, but because the risk was so serious and the consequences probably irreversible, the government decided to halt the project. That decision reflects what's known as the precautionary principle. When an action or policy poses a plausible risk of serious or irreversible harm, we shouldn't wait for absolute scientific certainty before deciding what to do. Why is this important? The consequences of indecision can be expensive or impossible to repair. Consider asbestos. Once a widely used material for insulation and fireproofing, it was later linked to life-threatening adverse effects on human health. But for decades, lack of consensus among researchers delayed government action, and by the time bans on asbestos were enacted, millions had been exposed, leading to costly litigation, cleanup, and preventable deaths.
