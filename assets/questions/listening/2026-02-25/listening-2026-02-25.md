### Listen to a Talk in a music class – Questions 25-28

audio: listening-talk1.m4a
image: 19.png

Professor: Have you ever listened to a piece of music and felt like you were transported somewhere else, maybe walking through a Brazilian street market, or climbing a misty mountain trail in China? That's the magic of musical borrowing, a practice where artists blend elements from different global traditions to create something fresh and emotionally resonant. Take bossa nova, for example. It's a genre that merges the laid back swing of United States jazz music with the rhythmic pulse of Brazilian samba. When you listen to bossa nova, it feels both familiar and new at the same time. But successful musical borrowing isn't just about mixing sounds, it's about respect. When artists draw from other cultures, they need to understand the traditions behind the music. Composer Tan Dun nails this in his score for the film Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon. He blends Chinese instruments like the erhu and the pipa with western orchestration. And not just superficially. The erhu's haunting tones and the pipa's intricate rhythms reflect centuries of Chinese musical storytelling. Tan Dun doesn't just borrow, he honors the culture, letting its emotional depth shine through. Of course, fusion isn't always seamless. Sometimes the mix feels forced, or the original meaning gets lost. But when it works, when the traditions truly speak to each other, it's proof that music can be a universal language, capable of bridging worlds and telling stories that transcend borders.

### Listen to a Talk in a marine biology class – Questions 29-32

audio: listening-talk2.m4a
image: 20.png

Professor: Let's talk a little about sea otters and their importance to their own and indeed other ecosystems. We'll start with a few basic facts. The sea otter is a marine mammal often found along the coastal waters of the northern Pacific. It lacks the blubber of other marine mammals, like whales or seals. But it stays warm in icy waters thanks to its fur, which is the densest of any animal's. It also has unusually large lungs, two and a half times larger than those of similarly sized land mammals, and this helps it stay buoyant and dive efficiently. Sea otters have a loose flap of skin under each forearm that functions like a pocket, which they use for storing food and tools. That's right, tools. Sea otters are one of the few animals that use tools, using clam shells for digging and rocks to open clam shells. Now, wonderful as they may be, why care about sea otters? Because sea otters eat sea urchins, and sea urchins eat kelp. Kelp is a large seaweed that can grow up to thirty meters tall, providing food and shelter for a range of marine life. Kelp forests support a greater variety of marine life than almost any other ocean habitat. Kelp also grows very densely and acts as a natural buffer, protecting shorelines from storm damage. Finally, kelp forests are like rain forests in that they absorb and store carbon dioxide through photosynthesis, mitigating climate change and reducing ocean acidification.

audio: listening-m2talk1.m4a
image: 26.png

Professor: Why do some companies thrive, while others with similar resources falter? Organizational agility might hold the key. Organizational agility is a company's ability to quickly adapt to market changes by making fast decisions, being flexible and responsive to customer needs, and being able to reconfigure internal structures when needed. Take an example you're probably familiar with. A streaming media company that began as a DVD rental service successfully pivoted to online streaming, just as internet speeds improved, and later moved into producing original content. These shifts were strategic and fast. They allowed the company to stay ahead of consumer expectations and industry trends. Agility also means responding to customer demands. A well-known clothing retailer, for instance, designed its supply chain to move new styles from concept to store in just a few weeks. If a celebrity wears a bold new look, this retailer can have a similar item on shelves while competitors are still in the design phase. That kind of readiness keeps the brand relevant. Agility comes with hurdles, like cultural resistance. In one case, a large industrial firm tried to adopt agile practices across its divisions. This meant forming cross-functional teams, groups made up of people from different departments like engineering, marketing, and operations, who would collaborate on shared goals. But many employees had spent decades in rigid hierarchies with clearly defined roles. Cross-functional teams felt chaotic and unfamiliar. This industrial firm had to invest in training, to help employees understand that agility wasn't about abandoning structure, but about building smarter, more responsive systems.

### Listen to a Talk in a biology class – Questions 12-15

audio: listening-m2talk2.m4a
image: 27.png

Professor: Trilobites were a group of ancient sea creatures. They're classified as arthropods. They're extinct now. Among modern animals, horseshoe crabs probably resemble trilobites the most. As arthropods, both trilobites and horseshoe crabs have segmented bodies, joints in their legs, and hard exoskeletons, kind of like shells. So we've known for years that trilobites had two compound eyes. There are different kinds of compound eyes, but for many trilobites it meant that their eyes had thousands of tiny lenses. This is helpful for detecting motion, like noticing other creatures swimming around. Other types of compound eyes provided, say, sharper images. But having thousands of lenses was most common for trilobites. But in a recent study, researchers examined a trilobite fossil that was damaged. Part of its head was missing. This allowed them to notice, in the middle of the trilobite's forehead, three tiny dark spots. They said, hmm, these don't seem random. In fact, the tiny spots are evidence of a median eye, that is, a third eye. Most arthropods have median eyes, which aren't really like compound eyes. They're simpler. But a median eye had never been found in trilobites before. Why? Well, researchers found that it was only there during the larval stage, early in the development of a young trilobite. So researchers thought adult fossils wouldn't have it. But it would have been hidden underneath a thin, dark layer of shell, which was missing in this fossil. So you wouldn't see it in a fossil whose entire head was intact.
