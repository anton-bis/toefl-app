### Listen to a Talk in an Anthropology Class – Questions 1-4

subtitle: Multispecies Anthropology
audio: listening_02_multispecies_anthropology.mp3
image: 2.png

For most of its history, the scientific field of anthropology, the study of culture, put humans at the center, often in ways that now seem male and limiting. Animals were treated as resources, symbols or mere background, important only for what they meant to us. Classic anthropology focused on how people hunted, herded or mythologized animals, reinforcing a sharp divide. Humans belonged to culture, animals to nature. For example, early studies of arctic hunting rituals examined how seals symbolized spiritual beliefs and social bonds without asking about the seals' own behaviors or ecological roles. This approach gave us insight into human meaning-making, but it overlooked the complexity of interspecies relationships. Multispecies anthropology flips that perspective. It sees humans and other species, animals, plants, even fungi, as co-participants in shared worlds. Instead of reducing non-humans to cultural symbols, it highlights their agency, their ability to act, adapt and influence human lives. Take the studies of mushroom foraging in Siberia. Here, fungi, trees and humans form interdependent networks. The growth of Matsutake mushrooms depends on and reflects forest health and human harvesting practices. Even as harvesting practices and forest health are influenced by the mushrooms themselves. The point is, life isn't a human story with animals or plants in the background. It's a web of relationships among many kinds of beings. Next, I'll explore how this multispecies perspective reshapes research methods. What it means to study not just human cultures, but the entangled lives of humans and other beings.

### Listen to a Talk in an Archaeology Class – Questions 1-4

subtitle: Cladh Hallan Mummies
audio: listening_04_cladh_hallan_mummies.mp3
image: 4.png

Often, when we think of mummies, we think of ancient Egyptians and their elaborate methods of preserving bodies. We used to think Egyptians were the only people practicing mummification during the Bronze Age, about, oh, four thousand years ago, because typical mummies are found in places, for example, with very dry climates, like Egypt. But then, archaeologists discovered a site in Scotland with evidence of mummification. And Scotland is, well, wet, rainy. This site is called Clad-Hallan, the only place in Britain where we found prehistoric Bronze Age mummies. So, researchers unearthed skeletons at Clad-Hallan, including a man and a woman from around 1500 BCE. Since these were just skeletons, the researchers didn't realise at first that they had actually been mummies. That realisation came after doing a test called radiocarbon dating on the bones. Radiocarbon dating revealed that the man and woman had died several hundred years before being buried. Normally, such a long weight would turn a body into a pile of bones. Not an actual skeleton, like what was found. So, their bodies must have been purposely preserved for hundreds of years before burial. Why? Well, who knows. But we do know how. The bodies were preserved. Mummified. In a peat bog. Peat bogs are kinda like swamps, except their conditions prevent organic materials, like bodies, from fully breaking down. They stay more intact. Prehistoric people in Scotland used to preserve animal fat, like butter, in peat bogs. So they definitely had the concept.

### Listen to a Talk in a Marketing Class – Questions 1-4

subtitle: Storytelling in Marketing
audio: listening_05_storytelling_in_marketing.mp3
image: 5.png

One of the most effective strategies in modern marketing is the use of storytelling. Storytelling involves crafting a narrative around a brand or product that engages consumers on an emotional level instead of simply listing features and benefits. Marketers use stories to create a connection with their audience, making the brand memorable and relatable. This method has been especially powerful in the age of social media where personal stories and viral content can significantly boost brand awareness and loyalty. Successful storytelling and marketing often includes elements like a compelling character, a relatable conflict, and a satisfying resolution. For example, a company selling eco-friendly products might tell the story of a family striving to reduce their environmental footprint. This narrative not only highlights the product's benefits, but also resonates with consumers who share similar values. Another key aspect of storytelling and marketing is authenticity. Consumers are more likely to trust and engage with brands that appear genuine and transparent. This means that the story should reflect the brand's real values and mission, avoiding exaggeration or false claims. Visuals also play a crucial role in storytelling. High quality images and videos can bring the narrative to life, making it more impactful and easier to share. Of course, measuring the success of storytelling campaigns can be difficult, as traditional metrics like sales figures don't always capture the full impact of a well-told story.
