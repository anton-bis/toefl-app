### Listen to a Talk in a marine biology class – Questions 25-28

audio: listening-talk1.m4a
image: 19.png

Professor: Let's talk a little about sea otters and their importance to their own and indeed other ecosystems. We'll start with a few basic facts. The sea otter is a marine mammal often found along the coastal waters of the northern Pacific. It lacks the blubber of other marine mammals, like whales or seals. But it stays warm in icy waters thanks to its fur, which is the densest of any animal's. It also has unusually large lungs, two and a half times larger than those of similarly sized land mammals, and this helps it stay buoyant and dive efficiently. Sea otters have a loose flap of skin under each forearm that functions like a pocket, which they use for storing food and tools. That's right, tools. Sea otters are one of the few animals that use tools, using clam shells for digging and rocks to open clam shells. Now, wonderful as they may be, why care about sea otters? Because sea otters eat sea urchins, and sea urchins eat kelp. Kelp is a large seaweed that can grow up to thirty meters tall, providing food and shelter for a range of marine life. Kelp forests support a greater variety of marine life than almost any other ocean habitat. Kelp also grows very densely and acts as a natural buffer, protecting shorelines from storm damage. Finally, kelp forests are like rain forests in that they absorb and store carbon dioxide through photosynthesis, mitigating climate change and reducing ocean acidification.

### Listen to a Talk in a literature class – Questions 29-32

audio: listening-talk2.m4a
image: 20.png

Professor: So today we're looking at the hero's journey, a storytelling pattern that shows up in myths, literature, and narratives from all over the world. This structure was brought into the spotlight by Joseph Campbell, an American mythologist. In his book, The Hero with a Thousand Faces, Campbell studied myths across cultures and noticed they shared a common structure. Campbell called it the monomyth, and he believed it reflected something universal about the human experience. The journey usually kicks off with a call to adventure, some kind of invitation or challenge that pulls the hero out of their everyday life. They might resist at first, but eventually they accept, often with a mentor's help. Then come the trials, tests that push the hero to grow. At the center of it all is the abyss, the toughest part, where the hero faces their greatest fear or enemy. If they make it through, they're transformed. And here's the key part. They return with a gift, some kind of insight, wisdom, or ability that can help others. That gift is what makes the journey meaningful, not just for the hero, but for their community. Now, some critics argue this model oversimplifies storytelling. It can flatten out cultural differences and force diverse stories into a single mold. Still, I'd argue it's influential because it taps into something deeply human: the idea that struggle leads to growth, and that we come back changed.

### Listen to a Talk in a psychology class – Questions 8-11

audio: listening-m2talk1.m4a
image: 26.png

Professor: The concept of flow is a fascinating area of study in psychology. It refers to a state of complete immersion and engagement in an activity. It is characterized by a sense of losing oneself in an activity, where time seems to disappear and everything feels effortless. This state is often associated with high levels of performance and satisfaction. Flow occurs when a person's skill level is perfectly matched to the challenge at hand, creating a balance that leads to deep focus and enjoyment. Athletes often experience flow during peak performance moments, when they are fully absorbed in the game and executing actions seamlessly. Artists also report flow states when they are deeply engaged in the creative process. There are several key components to achieving flow. First, the activity must have clear goals and immediate feedback, allowing individuals to understand their progress and adjust their actions accordingly. Second, the person must be fully focused on the activity, minimizing distractions and interruptions. Third, there must be a sense of control over the activity, with the individual feeling capable of meeting the challenges presented. Flow has numerous benefits, including increased productivity, enhanced creativity, and improved well-being. People who regularly experience flow are often happier and more fulfilled, as they spend time engaged in activities that are meaningful and enjoyable. However, achieving flow is not easy. It requires finding activities that match one's skill level to the appropriate level of challenge. Matching an activity with the requisite skill level can be challenging.

### Listen to a Talk in an environmental science class – Questions 12-15

audio: listening-m2talk2.m4a
image: 27.png

Professor: Today we are revisiting the topic of urban green spaces, green areas in cities like parks and gardens. These spaces are known to improve air quality and reduce heat in cities. They also support the health of city residents. Creating and maintaining urban green spaces can be challenging, however, especially in densely populated cities. An obvious issue is limited space in areas that are expensive or already developed. Finding room for parks or gardens can be difficult. One way cities address this is by building green roofs, a layer of vegetation planted on top of a building. Green roofs help cool buildings, absorb rainwater, and add greenery without taking up ground space. Cities also often repurpose unused lots, such as abandoned parking areas or vacant lots, turning them into small parks or gardens. Vertical gardens are another innovation. These are walls covered with plants, often using special structures that allow greenery to grow. They are useful in places where horizontal space is limited, and can improve air quality and insulation. While the cost and upkeep of green spaces are legitimate concerns, many cities are adopting green space quotas, policies that require a minimum amount of greenery in new developments. These quotas mandate that builders include features like trees, rooftop gardens, or courtyards. Though they may raise construction costs, urban planners support them because they ensure that green space is not overlooked in city growth. Quotas help balance development with environmental health, and are a step toward more sustainable urban living.
