### Listen to a Talk in a Biology Class – Questions 1-4

subtitle: Dynamic Mimicry
audio: Audio_01_Dynamic_Mimicry.mp3
image: 1.png

Professor: I once saw a video of a lionfish with its intimidating long spikes. Then it suddenly turned into a snake. Well, it turns out I was actually watching a mimic octopus.
Professor: The mimic octopus lives in relatively shallow ocean water, and it is the first animal known to show dynamic mimicry, which means it can shift quickly between multiple imitations.
Professor: Now, other creatures use mimicry. The owl butterfly's wings have markings resembling an owl's eye, which may keep the butterfly's predators away. But that is how the owl butterfly always looks. With dynamic mimicry, by contrast, an animal can shift its shape in real time.
Professor: It contorts its body into different formations, often to confuse predators. A mimic octopus can imitate a lionfish or a snake. And it can quickly extend its arms and glide across the sea floor like a flatfish.
Professor: But what use is changing shape if you cannot also change color to look like a flatfish? Well, this octopus has cells called chromatophores just under its skin. Each chromatophore has a pigment inside it that could be red, yellow, and so on.
Professor: Picture thousands of tiny balloons of different colors. If you stretch a balloon, it gets bigger, right? Well, the mimic octopus has muscles surrounding these chromatophores. They tighten to pull the necessary colors bigger and relax to make other colors smaller, making each color more or less visible.
Professor: While this helps it hide from predators, it also aids in hunting, as unsuspecting prey often perceives the octopus as a non-threatening species.

### Listen to a Talk in a Business Class – Questions 9-12

subtitle: Dark Stores
audio: Audio_04_Dark_Stores.mp3
image: 4.png

Professor: Today we are discussing the concept of dark stores. That is, retail centers that resemble warehouses and are usually located near city centers. Unlike traditional storefronts, they are not designed for walk-in customers, but function as fulfillment hubs optimized for rapid online delivery.
Professor: The rise of dark stores has been fueled by the growing demand for online shopping, especially in urban areas where space is limited and rent is high. The main appeal of dark stores lies in their efficiency. By eliminating the traditional storefront, businesses can reduce overhead costs and pass savings on to consumers through lower prices.
Professor: Their proximity to dense urban areas also allows for faster delivery times, meeting the expectations of customers who value speed and convenience. For instance, a company might deliver groceries in under an hour, giving it a major competitive advantage over traditional supermarkets.
Professor: However, dark stores are not without drawbacks. Their expansion can contribute to the decline of traditional retail spaces, creating economic challenges for local businesses. Employment is another concern. While these centers generate jobs in logistics and delivery, they may reduce the number of customer-facing roles available in physical stores.
Professor: Looking ahead, many experts predict that dark stores will continue to reshape the retail landscape. Some may adopt advanced automation, further reducing the need for human labor and potentially redefining the shopping experience itself. As with other technological shifts, the key challenge will be balancing efficiency with the broader social and economic implications of these changes.
