### Listen to a Talk in a Social Sciences Class – Questions 7-10

subtitle: The Creative Class and Urban Revitalization
audio: listening_m2_q12_q15_lecture_the_creative_class_and_urban_revitalization.mp3
image: 9.png

Have you ever wondered why certain urban neighborhoods flourish while others stagnate? A fascinating theory, known as the creative class, offers one explanation. Coined by sociologist Richard Florida, this concept suggests that cities with a high concentration of creative professionals, including artists, scientists, and entrepreneurs, tend to experience more significant economic growth and revitalization. The idea is that these individuals drive innovation and attract others like them, creating a vibrant dynamic atmosphere. However, it's important to note that such a transformation isn't without its drawbacks. When these creative hubs thrive economically, it can inadvertently lead to gentrification. That is, it can cause rising property values and living costs. This often displaces long-standing residents who can no longer afford to live in their own neighborhoods. This process can erode the unique cultural fabric that made the area attractive in the first place. Despite this, cities continue to pursue the creative class strategy. They invest in cultural infrastructure, like galleries and performance spaces, to lure creative professionals. Yet, critics argue that this focus on attracting the creative class might overshadow the needs of other community members, particularly those in lower income brackets. It's a delicate balancing act that cities must navigate carefully, fostering economic vitality while ensuring inclusivity and equity. So, next time you stroll through a bustling urban district filled with lively cafes and art installations, consider the multifaceted dynamics at play that underpin its success.

### Listen to a Talk in a Biology Class – Questions 15-18

subtitle: Monarch Butterfly Migration
audio: listening_m2_q08_q11_lecture_monarch_butterfly_migration.mp3
image: 11.png

The phenomenon of animal migration is a fascinating subject in biology. Migration involves the regular, often seasonal movement of animals from one region to another, typically in search of food, breeding grounds, or better climates. This behavior is observed in a wide variety of species, including birds, mammals, fish, and insects. One of the most well-known examples is the migration of monarch butterflies. These butterflies travel thousands of miles from North America to central Mexico to escape the cold winter. This journey involves multiple generations, as no single butterfly makes the round trip. Another remarkable example is the migration of humpback whales. These massive creatures travel from their feeding grounds in polar waters to warmer tropical regions where they breed and give birth. Birds like the Arctic turn undertake one of the longest migrations, traveling from the Arctic to the Antarctic and back each year. Despite their differences in size, species, and reasons for migrating, they all migrate vast distances with precision. They navigate like seasoned human pilots, flying transcontinental routes, only without aircraft, instruments, or GPS. Of course, migration is not without its challenges. Animals must navigate vast distances, often facing predators, adverse weather, and human-made obstacles like buildings and roads. Protecting habitats and reducing human interference can help maintain the delicate balance that allows migration to occur. Understanding animal migration provides insight into the complex behaviors and survival strategies of various species.

### Listen to a Talk in a Music History Class – Questions 27-30

audio: listening_m2_q12_q15_lecture_john_cage_and_the_prepared_piano.mp3
image: 14.png

Imagine taking a regular piano and transforming its sound by inserting everyday objects like screws, bolts and rubber erasers between the strings. This is what's known as a prepared piano. It was developed by the avant-garde composer John Cage in 1940. Cage was working at a university in Seattle and he needed to write music with percussion to accompany dancers. But the performance space's stage wasn't big enough for percussion instruments like drums, so he made the sounds with just the piano by placing objects between the strings. Now, Cage spent time searching for items that made the kind of sounds he was looking for. It had a feel of improvisation. Cage himself said that looking for items was kind of like walking on the beach and looking for shiny or beautiful seashells that you liked. That is, he didn't know ahead of time what items he wanted to use. He just kept looking around and seeing what suited his needs. Cage ended up composing a collection of music for prepared piano in the mid-1940s. He included specific instructions for how to prepare the piano. For this collection, Cage was inspired by ancient Hindu theory of about eight essential emotions. He wanted his listeners to sense eight essential emotions from Hindu philosophy. The result is a blending of sounds that would be familiar to a Western audience, plus sounds reminiscent of Indonesian gamelan. Gamelan is traditional Indonesian percussion music. This prepared piano music really raised John Cage's profile in the music world.

### Listen to a Talk in an Archaeology Class – Questions 31-34

subtitle: Gobekli Tepe
audio: listening_m2_q12_q15_lecture_gobekli_tepe.mp3
image: 15.png

For decades, archaeologists believed that complex societies and the monumental architecture, the large buildings signifying them, only emerged after agriculture and that farming was essential for societal development. After all, before agriculture developed, people tended not to settle in a single place but moved from place to place in search of food. We call such people nomadic hunter-gatherers. They were continuously on the move to hunt and collect food. And for people who aren't settled, it was more practical to live in small, mobile groups rather than in complex societies, or so it was believed. But the excavation of the ancient site of Gobekli Tepe threw a wrench in this whole story. Gobekli Tepe, dating back about 11,000 years, consists of multiple massive pillars arranged in circles and is considered the world's oldest known temple. Remarkably, it predates the earliest evidence of agriculture in the area by several thousand years. This suggests spiritual practices, not agriculture, may have caused early communities to form. Many scholars interpret the site as a ceremonial centre, a periodic gathering place for nomadic hunter-gatherers. More recently, archaeologists there did find traces of things like food processing areas, stone tools for grinding grains, as well as actual plant residues. Yet these residues come from local wild grains, not domesticated crops, and the structures may not indicate year-round habitation. So while Gobekli Tepe might have hosted temporary gatherings, the evidence still supports the idea that spiritual practices played a central role in early societal development.
