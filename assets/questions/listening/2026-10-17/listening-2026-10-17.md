### Listen to a Talk in a Chemistry Class – Questions 5-8

subtitle: The Accidental Discovery of Teflon
audio: listening_m1_q29_q32_lecture_the_accidental_discovery_of_teflon.mp3
image: 2.png

Back in 1938, a chemist named Roy Plunkett was working to create a new refrigerant, a new cooling substance. Plunkett was looking for an industrial refrigerant to replace the ones that existed at the time, which were toxic and flammable. Some, for example, could cause fatal poisoning if leaked. He was working with a gas called tetrafluoroethylene, or TFE, a highly flammable refrigerant. Plunkett stored TFE gas in cylinders, but one day he noticed that a cylinder of TFE no longer had gas in it. What was there? Well, powder. A solid. The TFE gas had solidified into this new powdery substance, polytetrafluoroethylene, or PTFE. What made PTFE remarkable was its almost unprecedented stability, its resistance to heat, chemicals, and friction. It reacted with other substances, making it ideal for industrial applications, like parts used in the aerospace industry, also medical devices and electronics. Eventually, PTFE began to be used in consumer products. Ever heard of Teflon, the non-stick coating on frying pans? That is PTFE, and it was used in homes around the world for many years. These days, you may be more likely to use stainless steel or cast iron cookware due to concerns with PTFE. Nevertheless, this is a classic example of serendipity in science. Plunkett wasn't trying to invent a non-stick surface, he stumbled upon it. This kind of discovery isn't rare. Scientists discovered x-rays, penicillin, and many other scientific and medical innovations while trying to do something else.

### Listen to a Talk in a Business Class – Questions 13-16

subtitle: Lean Manufacturing
audio: listening_m1_q29_q32_lecture_lean_manufacturing.mp3
image: 4.png

Let's talk about lean manufacturing and its impact on efficiency in production processes. Lean manufacturing is a systematic approach to minimizing waste within a manufacturing system while maximizing productivity. While the methodology has been adopted by many companies worldwide, it was developed by a Japanese car maker in the mid-20th century. But its roots can actually be traced to the supermarket. In the 1950s, Japanese engineers observed that supermarkets in the United States only restocked items as customers purchased them, maintaining just enough inventory to meet demand. This idea of just-in-time inventory management became a core principle of lean manufacturing. By identifying and removing excess inventory and eliminating other inefficiencies, companies could reduce costs and increase their overall productivity. Another important aspect of lean manufacturing is continuous improvement, often referred to as Kaizen. This involves constantly seeking ways to enhance processes, products and services. Employees at all levels are encouraged to contribute ideas for improvement, fostering a culture of innovation and teamwork. Lean manufacturing also emphasizes the importance of value from the customer's perspective. Companies focus on producing goods that meet customer needs and expectations, ensuring that each step in the production process adds value rather than unnecessary complexity. As you might imagine, implementing lean manufacturing requires a shift in organizational culture. Resistance to change and difficulty in sustaining improvements can get in the way. That said, companies that successfully adopt lean practices experience increased efficiency, reduced costs and higher customer satisfaction.

### Listen to a Talk on a Psychology Podcast – Questions 21-24

subtitle: Group Pressure and Social Influence
audio: listening_m1_q25_q28_lecture_group_pressure_and_social_influence.mp3
image: 6.png

There are many different factors that influence human behavior. One is related to social influence, or the idea that our behavior can be shaped by group pressure. To test this idea, a psychologist named Solomon Ash conducted a famous study in the 1950s which has become hugely influential in the field. In his experiment, Ash invited American university students to take part in a simple visual judgment task. Each participant was placed in a group of seven to nine people, all but one of whom were working with the researcher. The group members were shown a standard line on one card, and three comparison lines on another card. One comparison line matched the standard, while the other two were clearly different lengths. Basically, the correct choice was totally obvious. Each person announced aloud which line matched the standard, and those who were in on the study deliberately gave the wrong answer first. The real participant answered last. Across 12 trials, 75% of participants conformed with the incorrect answers that people in the group had given before them at least once. So they chose the line that was clearly wrong. This indicates strong influence of group pressure. This study was replicated in 1980, roughly 30 years after Ash conducted his experiment. The subjects in this experiment were British university students. Interestingly, conformity was much lower with these participants. This suggests that conformity may be influenced by culture and the country people grow up in.

### Listen to a Talk on a Sociology Podcast – Questions 25-28

subtitle: Sociograms and Social Research Methods
audio: listening_m1_q29_q32_lecture_sociograms_and_social_research_methods.mp3
image: 7.png

Social networks are not just digital online platforms. They're fundamental to understanding how people connect and interact. One powerful tool for analyzing these networks is the sociogram. A sociogram is a visual map that charts relationships within a group. It looks a little like a spider's web. Sociograms illustrate who interacts with whom, how frequently, and in what patterns. And by making invisible social structures visible, they reveal dynamics such as influence, isolation, and centrality. Rather than focusing on individual behavior as traditional studies would, sociograms emphasize the web of connections that shape group outcomes. In classrooms, they can identify students who are socially isolated or those who act as bridges between different social groups. In workplaces, sociograms help uncover informal hierarchies and where communication breaks down. In public health, they're used to trace contact patterns and predict how diseases might spread from person to person. Sociograms are valuable in marketing as well, where understanding who influences purchasing decisions can guide advertising strategy. However, sociograms do have limitations. By reducing relationships to lines in a web of interactions, they may overlook the subtleties of human interaction, such as trust, conflict, or emotional intensity that aren't easily captured in a diagram. Understanding these qualitative aspects often requires complementary methods like interviews or observation to fully appreciate. Still, when used thoughtfully and alongside other tools, sociograms remain a vital resource for visualizing and analyzing social networks. Their strength lies in turning abstract social dynamics into concrete, interpretable visuals, making them indispensable across disciplines.

subtitle: Ancient Hawaiian Coastal Fish Ponds
audio: listening_m2_q08_q11_lecture_ancient_hawaiian_coastal_fish_ponds.mp3
image: 9.png

Can food plus waste products trigger algal blooms? Algal blooms are rapid increases in algae, tiny aquatic plants that can cloud the water. When those algal blooms die off, they decompose and deplete oxygen from the water, which can suffocate fish and other aquatic life. Of course, the main cause of algal blooms is chemical fertilizers and agricultural runoff, like when water from rain or irrigation flows over fields and carries the fertilizers to rivers and coastal waters. But fish farming causes algal blooms too. An intriguing alternative is or was the coastal fish ponds of ancient Hawaii called Lokoia. People in Hawaii are reviving them today. These ponds, built along the shore using mainly lava, capture nutrient-rich runoff from nearby fields, naturally fertilizing the ponds. This was before the days of concentrated artificial fertilizers. Instead of causing algal blooms, the nutrients supported modest growth of algae, which fed local plant-eating fish. The ponds used natural tidal flushing to bring in fresh seawater and remove waste, which kept oxygen level stable. And here's the clever part. The special sluice gates connecting the ponds to the ocean had small holes. These holes allowed juvenile fish to swim in from the ocean and fatten up on the food in the pond, but then prevented them from escaping once they matured, so they could be easily harvested.
