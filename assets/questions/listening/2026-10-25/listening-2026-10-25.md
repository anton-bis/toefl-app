audio: listening_m2_q12_q15_lecture_galapagos_tortoise_relocation.mp3
image: 6.png

The Galapagos Islands, a popular tourist attraction located approximately 1,000 kilometers off mainland Ecuador, have long been home to many interesting species of animals, including giant tortoises. Historically these reptiles could be found on many different Galapagos Islands, but in some of these locations they've gone extinct, due in part to human activities. To deal with this situation, conservationists employed a strategy called Animal Translocation, to move tortoises from one island to another. So why did conservationists do this? The main reason was to promote ecosystem recovery on affected islands. One place tortoises went extinct was on Santa Fe Island. When these reptiles died out, the island was without its main ecosystem engineer. This affected the island in many ways, including causing plant diversity to decline. To help the ecosystem recover, conservationists have released more than 400 tortoises from Espanola Island onto Santa Fe Island. This was done to help restore natural processes like seed dispersal and vegetation control. The goal is to reach about 4,000 individuals to match historical densities, and there are signs that this project is having success. For example, a type of cactus that many animals on the island rely on for food has shown improved reproduction and growth since the tortoises returned. This is no doubt because tortoises eat this cactus's fruit and spread seeds in their nutrient-rich droppings. This finding is encouraging, but we can't declare the project a victory yet. More time needs to pass before conservationists can deem it a success.

Incandescent bulbs, though less efficient, might offer physiological benefits that LEDs lack. Despite these limitations, LED bulbs remain a popular and practical choice. But the question is, are the downsides worth it? Many experts say no, and they're advocating instead for advanced LED systems that bring near-infrared back into the mix. Such systems do exist, but they're pricey, sometimes ten times more than regular LEDs. So, for now at least, they're mostly used in specialized medical settings.

### Listen to a Talk in an Architecture Class – Questions 21-24

subtitle: Eastgate Centre and Termite-Inspired Architecture
audio: listening_m2_q08_q11_lecture_eastgate_centre_and_termite_inspired_architecture.mp3
image: 10.png

The East Gate Center in Harare Zimbabwe is a mixed use complex combining retail spaces and offices. When the architect, Mick Pierce, was asked to design the building in the early 1990s, he faced a big challenge. How to keep all its shops and offices cool without traditional air conditioning, and do it during an economic crisis. Energy costs were high and importing advanced air conditioners was impossible. To solve this dilemma, Pierce looked to termite mounds. Termites are insects that live in large colonies. They build big mounds on the ground throughout the African savanna. The mounds are amazing structures. Some rise three meters above ground and extend another one to three meters below the ground. The underground chambers connect to the above ground chimneys through a maze of tunnels. And this entire system works like natural climate control. Warm air rises and escapes through the top while cooler air flows in from below. The thick walls of soil and clay absorb heat during the day and release it at night. Termites fine tune air flow by opening and closing vents, adding or removing soil to enlarge or seal passages. On hot days, they open more vents for cooling. At night, they close them to keep warmth in. Pierce borrowed these ideas. The East Gate Center's walls are made from thick materials like concrete and brick, and there's a system of ducts and adjustable louvers throughout the building to mimic the vents. The result? A building that uses far less energy than its conventional counterparts.

Today, let's dive into a cornerstone of modern marketing, market segmentation. Picture a bustling town square. What makes it lively? Is it the buildings, the people, the signage? Sociologists argue that public spaces hold a mirror to a society's values. This concept, known as public sphere theory, suggests that the way we design and use our public spaces reveals much about our cultural identity. According to this theory, a city's parks, plazas and streetscapes serve not just as physical locations, but also as stages for social interaction and cultural exchange. The theory also examines how accessible these spaces are to the people who live near them. Another aspect of public sphere theory is the struggle faced by cities to balance the need for open, accessible areas with issues like overcrowding and other safety concerns. In addition, city planners often face the dilemma of preserving cultural heritage while adapting to modern needs of a community. I should note that technology is also reshaping public spaces. Throughout the years, archaeologists have discovered many fascinating artifacts on the Greek island of Crete. One example is the Feistos disk, an ancient clay artifact that was found in the ruins of the Minoan palace at Feistos. The Minoan civilization lasted from about 3100 BCE to 1100 BCE, and we think this artifact was made in the 2nd millennium BCE. It was discovered in 1908, and it features many stamped symbols, 241 to be exact, in the shape of a spiral, the symbols. They include images of things like animals, tools, and so on. Some suggest they could represent an unknown language. Others think they might represent a calendar or even a religious hymn. Yet no consensus has been reached. Another intriguing thing about the Feistos disk is the method used to imprint the symbols. This involved pressing prefabricated stamps into wet clay. This process is really remarkable. It suggests a level of sophistication similar to early printing. Some scholars hypothesize that the disk might be an import from another culture, a lone artifact brought to Crete by traders or travellers. But this is another point of contention surrounding this fascinating artifact.
