### Listen to a Talk on a Physics Podcast – Questions 25-28

subtitle: Ice Floats
audio: listening_m1_q25_q28_podcast_ice_floats.mp3
image: 19.png

Have you ever wondered why ice floats on water? It's a curious phenomenon that has puzzled scientists for centuries. Normally, solids are denser than liquids because their molecules are packed more closely together. But water has a unique molecular structure, and it behaves differently. As water molecules cool and form ice, they arrange themselves in a crystal structure that occupies more space than when they are in liquid form. This arrangement causes ice to be less dense than liquid water, enabling it to float. But why is this important beyond just curiosity? Imagine a world where ice sank. Lakes and ponds would freeze from the bottom up, and this could have a huge impact on aquatic ecosystems. Aquatic life relies on ice forming at the surface, which creates an insulating layer that prevents the entire body of water from freezing. This insulation is vital for the survival of fish and other animals during harsh winters. Even if the top layer is frozen, they can survive in the water underneath. Floating ice, especially in polar regions, also reflects a significant amount of sunlight back into space. This helps regulate global temperatures. If ice sank, it would disappear beneath the water, allowing more solar energy to be absorbed by the darker ocean or lake surfaces. This would lead to warmer water temperatures, accelerating global warming and disrupting climate patterns worldwide.

### Listen to a Talk in a Music Class – Questions 29-32

subtitle: Musical Borrowing
audio: listening_m1_q29_q32_lecture_musical_borrowing.mp3
image: 20.png

Have you ever listened to a piece of music and felt like you were suddenly somewhere else? Maybe walking through a Brazilian street market or climbing a misty mountain trail in China? That's the magic of musical borrowing, a practice where artists blend elements from different global traditions to create something fresh and emotionally resonant. Take bossa nova, for example. It's a genre that merges the laid-back swing of United States jazz music with the rhythmic pulse of Brazilian samba. When you listen to bossa nova, it feels both familiar and new at the same time. But successful musical borrowing isn't just about mixing sounds. It's about respect. When artists draw from other cultures, they need to understand the meanings behind the music. Composers like Tan Dun nail this in his score for the film Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon. He blends Chinese instruments like the arrow and pipa with Western orchestration, but not just for effect. The arrows haunting tones and the pipa's intricate rhythms reflect centuries of Chinese musical storytelling. Tan Dun doesn't just borrow. He honors the culture, letting its emotional depth shine through. Of course, fusion isn't always seamless. Sometimes the mix feels forced, or the original meaning gets lost. But when it works, when the traditions truly speak to each other, it's proof that music can be a universal language capable of bridging worlds and telling stories that transcend borders.

### Listen to a Talk in an Environmental Science Class – Questions 8-11

subtitle: Urban Green Spaces
audio: listening_m2_q08_q11_lecture_urban_green_spaces.mp3
image: 26.png

Today, we're revisiting the topic of urban green spaces, green areas in cities like parks and gardens. These spaces are known to improve air quality and reduce heat in cities. They also support the health of city residents. Creating and maintaining urban green spaces can be challenging, however, especially in densely populated cities. An obvious issue is space. In areas where land is expensive or already developed, finding room for parks or gardens can be difficult. One way cities address this is by building green roofs. That's a layer of vegetation planted on top of a building. Green roofs help cool buildings, absorb rainwater, and add greenery without taking up ground space. Cities also often repurpose and used lot, such as abandoned parking areas or vacant lot, turning them into small parks or gardens. Vertical gardens are another innovation. These are walls covered with plants, often using special structures that allow greenery to grow upward. They're useful in places where horizontal space is limited and can improve air quality and insulation. Of course, cost and upkeep of green spaces are legitimate concerns. Many cities are adopting green space quotas, policies that require a minimum amount of greenery in new development. Quotas mandate builders to include features like trees, rooftop gardens, or courtyards. Though they may raise construction costs, many urban planners support them because they ensure that green space is not overlooked in city growth. Quotas help balance development with environmental health and are a step toward more sustainable urban living.

### Listen to a Talk on a Personal Health Podcast – Questions 12-15

subtitle: Biohacking
audio: listening_m2_q12_q15_lecture_biohacking.mp3
image: 27.png

Let's talk about biohacking. Biohacking is an increasingly popular self-directed approach to enhancing physical and mental performance, often through methods that lack full scientific support. One widely practiced biohacking technique is intermittent fasting, where people restrict eating to only specific windows of time. The practice is believed to help regulate energy levels and support metabolic efficiency. Although research is ongoing, results so far do show some metabolic benefits. Certain types of substances that are considered cognitive enhancers are another cornerstone of biohacking. Green tea extract, for example, is intended to improve memory, attention, and focus. Users often keep track of their productivity, mood, and memory to refine their intake. However, long-term data about the effects of green tea extract and other cognitive enhancers are often lacking. Such gaps emphasize the need for more rigorous large-scale studies to determine their safety and effectiveness. Tracking sleep patterns is also central to biohacking. Wearable devices can monitor sleep stages, heart rate, and movement. This can help users identify patterns and make changes to improve sleep quality, such as adjusting room temperature in evening routines. Some people use sound skates, for example ocean waves or forest sounds, to influence brainwave activity and deepen their sleep. Light plays a role in sleep, too. Less nighttime light from digital screens and more bright light in the morning help improve one's sleep wake cycle. The biohacking method that seems most strongly supported by research is manipulating light exposure, since it does appear to significantly affect sleep quality.
