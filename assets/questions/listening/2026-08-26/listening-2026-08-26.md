### Listen to a Talk on a Physics Podcast – Questions 25-28

subtitle: Ice Floats
audio: listening_m1_q25_q28_lecture_ice_floats.mp3
image: 19.png

Have you ever wondered why ice floats on water? It's a curious phenomenon that has puzzled scientists for centuries. Normally solids are denser than liquids because their molecules are packed more closely together. But water has a unique molecular structure and it behaves differently. As water molecules cool and form ice, they arrange themselves in a crystalline structure that occupies more space than when they are in liquid form. This arrangement causes ice to be less dense than liquid water, enabling it to float. But why is this important beyond just curiosity? Well, imagine a world where ice sank. Lakes and ponds would freeze from the bottom up and this could have a huge impact on aquatic ecosystems. Aquatic life relies on ice forming at the surface, which creates an insulating layer that prevents the entire body of water from freezing. This insulation is vital for the survival of fish and other animals during harsh winters. Even if the top layer is frozen, they can survive in the water underneath. Floating ice, especially in polar regions, also reflects a significant amount of sunlight back into space. This helps regulate global temperatures. If ice sank, it wouldn't remain on the surface to reflect sunlight. It would disappear beneath the water, allowing more solar energy to be absorbed by the darker ocean or lake surfaces. This would lead to warmer water temperatures, accelerating global warming and disrupting climate patterns worldwide.

### Listen to a Talk in a Business Class – Questions 29-32

subtitle: Dark Stores
audio: listening_m1_q29_q32_lecture_dark_stores.mp3
image: 20.png

Today we're discussing the concept of dark stores. That is, retail centers that resemble warehouses and are usually located near city centers. Unlike traditional storefronts, they are not designed for walk-in customers, but function as fulfillment hubs, optimized for rapid online delivery. The rise of dark stores has been fueled by the growing demand for online shopping, especially in urban areas where space is limited and rent is high. The main appeal of dark stores lies in their efficiency. By eliminating the traditional storefront, businesses can reduce overhead costs and pass savings onto consumers through lower prices. Their proximity to dense urban areas also allows for faster delivery times, meeting the expectations of customers who value speed and convenience. For instance, a company might deliver groceries in under an hour, giving it a major competitive advantage over traditional supermarkets. However, dark stores are not without drawbacks. Their expansion can contribute to the decline of traditional retail spaces, creating economic challenges for local businesses. Employment is another concern. While these centers generate jobs in logistics and delivery, they may reduce the number of customer-facing roles available in physical stores. Looking ahead, many experts predict that dark stores will continue to reshape the retail landscape. Some may adopt advanced automation, further reducing the need for human labor and potentially redefining the shopping experience itself. As with other technological shifts, the key challenge will be balancing efficiency with the broader social and economic implications of these changes.

### Listen to a Talk in an Anthropology Class – Questions 8-11

subtitle: Multispecies Anthropology
audio: listening_m2_q08_q11_lecture_multispecies_anthropology.mp3
image: 26.png

For most of its history, the scientific field of anthropology, the study of culture, put humans at the center, often in ways that now seem narrow and limiting. Animals were treated as resources, symbols, or mere background, important only for what they meant to us. Classic anthropology focused on how people hunted, herded, or mythologized animals, reinforcing a sharp divide. Humans belonged to culture, animals, to nature. For example, early studies of arctic hunting rituals examined how seals symbolized spiritual beliefs and social bonds without asking about the seal's own behaviors or ecological roles. This approach gave us insight into human meaning-making, but it overlooked the complexity of interspecies relationships. Multispecies anthropology flips that perspective. It sees humans and other species, animals, plants, even fungi, as co-participants in shared worlds. Instead of reducing nonhumans to cultural symbols, it highlights their agency, their ability to act, adapt, and influence human lives. Take the studies of mushroom foraging in Siberia. Here, fungi, trees, and humans form interdependent networks. The growth of Matsutake mushrooms depends on and reflects forest health and human harvesting practices. Even as harvesting practices and forest health are influenced by the mushrooms themselves. The point is, life isn't a human story with animals or plants in the background. It's a web of relationships among many kinds of beings. Next, I'll explore how this multispecies perspective reshapes research methods, what it means to study not just human cultures, but the entangled lives of humans and other beings.

### Listen to a Talk on a Science Podcast – Questions 12-15

subtitle: Gas Emission Craters
audio: listening_m2_q12_q15_lecture_gas_emission_craters.mp3
image: 27.png

When you think of Siberia, you picture a cold place. But in 2014, Siberia had an unusually warm summer. The temp succeeded 30 degrees Celsius a couple times. And also in 2014, a helicopter pilot flying over a particular area of Siberia spotted a strange crater, a cylindrical hole in the ground that was massive, 20 meters wide, and 50 meters deep. Really huge. Following the report, scientists investigated and found more such craters in the same area. It looked like stuff had just exploded from the ground, with material flying out and landing hundreds of meters away. These explosions occurred in an area where oil and gas extraction take place, so they're a real concern. What if an explosion happens near an oil or gas pipeline? What's happening is connected to the warmer temperatures. Let's back up. The region's soil is permanently frozen. It's called permafrost, with pockets of ice in it. This permafrost is on top of an extensive field of underground natural gas. The ice and permafrost help hold the gas under the surface. But warming temperatures cause melting at the surface, so it can't seal the gas underground as effectively. Also, when the ice in the permafrost melts, it leaves behind empty cavities, where the ice used to be. What's probably happening is the gas from below rises up and fills these cavities, building up the pressure. Eventually, the gas bursts through. And boom, a crater causing explosion. These craters are called gas emission craters.
