### Listen to a Talk in a Physics Class - Questions 25-28

audio: listening_m1_q25_q28.m4a
image: 19.png

We've been talking about the law of inertia. An object moving in a straight line will continue to move in a straight line unless it is acted upon by another force. So what does this mean in the real world? Let's look at something many people do all the time. Riding a bicycle. Think about riding along in a straight line. Once the bike is moving, inertia will keep it going in a straight line. But we don't just ride in straight lines, do we? We turn at corners and steer around things that are in our way. So how do you get the bicycle not to go straight? Easy, right? Don't you just need to turn the front wheel in the direction you want to turn? In fact, if you only turn the front wheel, you're likely to be up on the pavement. The wheel will turn, but because of inertia, your body and the rest of the bike will want to keep moving straight ahead. So how does a cyclist avoid this? The key is to lean in. Weight needs to shift in the direction of the turn so that the force of gravity can work against the force of inertia. Professional cyclists who move at high speeds need to hit a sweet spot. They must lean more to keep their bicycles from sliding out of turns, but not too much. At this point, gravity will bring both rider and bicycle down to the ground.

If you've ever looked at a Picasso painting and thought, wait, is that a face or a guitar? You've already encountered the magic of cubism. This revolutionary movement shook up the art scene in the early 20th century. It wasn't just a new style. It was a complete reimagining of how we see and represent reality. Artists like Pablo Picasso and George Brach broke away from the rules of perspective and instead chose to depict objects from multiple angles at once. Imagine seeing every side of a coffee cup. Top, bottom and side, all in a single image. How this coffee cup is depicted. That's the kind of visual remix cubism brought to the canvas. At first, people didn't know what to make of it. Critics called it chaotic, even nonsensical. But over time, cubism proved to be a game-changing, influencing not just painting and sculpture, but also music, literature and architecture. It encouraged creators to rethink structure, narrative and form and to reconsider how we think about representation and abstraction. And it paved the way for other art movements, like modernism. Modernism also embraced abstraction and conceptual depth, though in somewhat different ways.

### Listen to a Talk in a Psychology Class - Questions 8-11

audio: listening_m2_q08_q11.m4a
image: 26.png

Have you ever wondered why certain songs evoke strong emotions or memories? This phenomenon is rooted in the music psychology, which examines how music impacts our feelings, thoughts and behaviours. Research indicates that music can trigger a range of emotional responses through its rhythm, melody and lyrics. For example, upbeat songs often make us feel happy and energetic. On the other hand, slow melancholic tunes can evoke sadness or nostalgia. But why does this happen? One theory suggests music stimulates the brain's reward system. That is, it releases a chemical called dopamine. Dopamine is a neurotransmitter associated with pleasure and motivation. In addition, music is often intertwined with personal experiences. Hearing a song that played during a significant life event can bring back vivid memories and the emotions associated with them. I should point out, though, that individual responses to music can vary greatly. Idiosyncratic factors that play a role in how we perceive and react to different types of music include cultural background, personality and the context in which we hear the song. Now, this effect that music has on emotional well-being is the basis for music therapy, which has been useful in treating a variety of psychological conditions like stress or anxiety. By leveraging music's emotional power, music therapy can help patients reduce stress and improve their overall well-being. More research is needed, but our growing understanding of music psychology points to its even greater benefits for mental health and emotional regulation in the future.

### Listen to a Talk in a History Class - Questions 12-15

audio: listening_m2_q12_q15.m4a
image: 27.png

Before we say any more about textile production, let's talk about the historical indigo trade. Indigo is a natural blue dye coming from the leaves of indigo fara plants. Today you see it most often in blue jeans. Indigo was a staple export for hundreds of years. There was a time when only rice was more valuable. The English began importing it during the early 1600s from India and British colonists started growing it in North America about a century later. Natural indigo nearly vanished from industrial use by the early 20th century after German chemists invented a synthetic indigo. Of course, this was cheaper and more efficient in scale production. And for decades, the natural stuff survived only in niche applications and local traditions. Today, indigo is back as a viable commercial crop in places like Bangladesh and the US. This is partly thanks to the fashion industry's efforts to be more environmentally sustainable and many now see natural indigo as a symbol of ethical consumption. Its value as a commodity also lies in its versatility. It's also used in cosmetics and food coloring. Farmers find it to be low maintenance with high reductions. One hectare can yield thousands of kilograms of leaves. The economic incentive is clear. Minimal input, high output, and growing international interest. That said, the traditional fermentation techniques for extracting the dye are extremely labor intensive, which makes natural indigo expensive. And some producers claiming natural methods still rely on harsh chemicals, which undermines the dye's eco-friendly reputation.
