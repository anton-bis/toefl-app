### Listen to a Talk in a Business Class – Questions 25-28

subtitle: Dark Stores
audio: listening_m1_q25_q28_lecture_dark_stores.mp3
image: 19.png

Today we're discussing the concept of dark stores. That is, retail centers that resemble warehouses and are usually located near city centers. Unlike traditional storefronts, they are not designed for walk-in customers, but function as fulfillment hubs optimized for rapid online delivery. The rise of dark stores has been fueled by the growing demand for online shopping, especially in urban areas where space is limited and rent is high. The main appeal of dark stores lies in their efficiency. By eliminating the traditional storefront, businesses can reduce overhead costs and pass savings on to consumers through lower prices. Their proximity to dense urban areas also allows for faster delivery times, meeting the expectations of customers who value speed and convenience. For instance, a company might deliver groceries in under an hour, giving it a major competitive advantage over traditional supermarkets. However, dark stores are not without drawbacks. Their expansion can contribute to the decline of traditional retail spaces, creating economic challenges for local businesses. Employment is another concern. While these centers generate jobs in logistics and delivery, they may reduce the number of customer-facing roles available in physical stores. Looking ahead, many experts predict that dark stores will continue to reshape the retail landscape. Some may adopt advanced automation, further reducing the need for human labor and potentially redefining the shopping experience itself. As with other technological shifts, the key challenge will be balancing efficiency with the broader social and economic implications of these changes.

### Listen to a Talk in a History Class – Questions 29-32

subtitle: Storage Furniture
audio: listening_m1_q29_q32_lecture_storage_furniture.mp3
image: 20.png

During the Roman Empire, soldiers had a lot of weapons and armor, and they needed a place to store them, their arms and armor. Soldiers used boxes called armariums to store this gear. Armariums were big, portable wooden storage boxes. Later, people, including civilians, kept using armariums in their homes, but to store items like clothing, books, or other valuables. In France in the 16th century, wealthy people had them and called them armoires. Armoire is a term we still use today for a large standalone piece of wooden furniture for storing things. Around the same time, some homes in England started to have what they called closets. But these closets were more like small studies where people could store things or also just read or be alone. Someone might play their guitar in one to relax. So both the armoire and the English closet, they were kind of precursors to the modern closet. If we fast forward to the mid-1800s in the early United States, we're starting to see closets that are built directly into homes as part of the floor plan. The big benefit of these was that they were less pricey than armoires. Way cheaper. People had less clothing then than they do today, and they'd usually use a small chest to store clothes or hang them on a peg or hook. Coat hangers weren't invented until the late 1800s. But still, closets in the home were handy places to store other belongings.

subtitle: Venus Lightning
audio: listening_m2_q08_q11_lecture_venus_lightning.mp3
image: 26.png

Today I've got a topic that might just electrify your imagination. Lightning in the atmosphere of Venus. You might wonder why we should care about lightning on another planet. Well, understanding lightning on Venus could give us clues about the planet's dynamic weather systems. Okay, so the Soviet Venera space missions set out to explore Venus, one of Earth's closest neighbors. What these missions found was surprising. Evidence of lightning in Venus' thick, sulfuric acid-laden clouds. So picture this. The sky is perpetually cloaked in clouds. Lightning flickers through the gloom. It's practically the setting for a science fiction movie. But this isn't fiction. It's real science. And here's where it gets interesting. The presence of lightning suggests that the atmosphere of Venus is more dynamic than we once assumed. Why does this matter? Lightning can play a crucial role in atmospheric chemistry, potentially affecting the formation of molecules and influencing weather patterns. On Earth, lightning is known to create compounds like nitrogen oxides, which are essential for plant life. While Venus doesn't have plant life, understanding its atmospheric processes helps scientists build models that can be applied to other planets, including our own. Moreover, studying Venusian lightning can help us predict future changes in its atmosphere. If lightning is as prevalent as the Venera missions suggest, it could mean that Venus is more geologically active than previously assumed. This suggests new ideas for research. Could other planets with similar conditions harbor surprising phenomena? Or even forms of life?

### Listen to a Talk in a Biology Class – Questions 12-15

subtitle: Honey Bees
audio: listening_m2_q12_q15_lecture_honey_bees.mp3
image: 27.png

Listen to part of a lecture in a biology class. Honeybees are social insects, forming highly organized colonies where teamwork is essential. Inside a hive, most bees are workers, but different workers have different jobs. For example, some specialize in maintaining the hive, while others head out to forage for food like nectar and pollen. Now researchers wanted to know if forager bees shared their workload evenly among themselves, or did some work harder than others. To find out, they tagged thousands of foragers with tiny microtransponders. Think of it like giving each bee its own identification badge, and they tracked every trip in and out of the hive. The results were surprising. About 20% of the forager bees made more than half of all the trips. These harder-working forager bees, called elite foragers, were foraging non-stop, while many others spent most of their time inside the hive. Interesting, right? But the researchers also wanted to know whether or not this hard-working behavior was an intrinsic permanent characteristic. To find out, the scientists ran a second experiment. They removed most of the elite foragers from the hive. And what happened? Initially, overall foraging activity dropped, which makes sense. But a day later, foragers that had been relatively inactive suddenly stepped up and started foraging like crazy. The colony bounced back quickly. Apparently, those less active foragers functioned like a reserve workforce, with each bee monitoring the colony's needs and adjusting its activity level accordingly. It's a wonderful example of flexibility, with individual bees working in concert to ensure the hive's survival.
