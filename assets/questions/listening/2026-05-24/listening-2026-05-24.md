### Listen to a Talk in a Biology Class – Questions 1-4

subtitle: Composting
audio: L3.mp3
image: 3.png

Human activities generate large amounts of organic waste, like food scraps, yard waste, and paper products. This waste can build up in landfills, but there it decomposes anaerobically, meaning without oxygen. Anaerobic decomposition results in the release of methane, which significantly contributes to global warming. But we have an alternative to landfills: composting. In composting, organic waste is broken down under special conditions by microorganisms, fungi, and sometimes insects. These organisms break down organic material into a dark, crumbly, earthy-smelling substance called humus. Unlike decomposition in landfills, composting occurs aerobically, with oxygen, and therefore doesn't produce methane gas. The compost pile needs a good balance of four things. One is nitrogen-rich material, also known as greens, like fruit and vegetable scraps. The other is carbon-rich material, or browns, like dry leaves, wood chips, and paper waste. And you also need water and air. Humus, the primary product of composting, is full of essential nutrients, meaning it enriches soil, enhances plant growth, and increases agricultural yields. This reduces the need for chemical fertilizers. Humus-enriched soil also supports beneficial microorganisms, insects, and worms that in turn help decompose organic material and maintain soil health. This creates a virtuous circle. In contrast, chemical fertilizers can boost agricultural productivity in the short term, but they are harmful to these beneficial organisms. They can actually damage the soil in the long run by killing the helpful organisms living in it.

### Listen to a Talk in a Literature Class – Questions 1-4

subtitle: Isolation in Literature
audio: L4.mp3
image: 4.png

A common theme in literature is isolation, and that's what we'll explore today. Isolation can be a powerful motif reflecting the psychological effects of being separated from society. One of the most classic examples of isolation in literature is Mary Shelley's Frankenstein. In this novel, Victor Frankenstein's grotesque creation, The Creature, experiences profound isolation due to his monstrous appearance and the fear he instills in people. This isolation leads to anger and a desire for revenge, thus highlighting the detrimental effects of being ostracized. Another notable example is The Catcher in the Rye by JD Salinger. The protagonist, Holden Caulfield, feels disconnected from the world around him, and he struggles with alienation and loneliness. This social isolation is what drives Holden's actions and internal conflicts throughout the book. Yet another example of isolation in literature, and one that ends badly, is Herman Melville's Moby Dick. In this novel, Captain Ahab's obsessive vengeful quest for the White Whale leads to his isolation. This obsession distances him from his crew as well as his humanity, and it propels him and his ship toward a destructive end. However, the portrayal of isolation in literature is not always negative. In Charlotte Bronte's Jane Eyre, the titular character experiences isolation at various points in her life, such as when she is sent to live with her unkind relatives, and later when she experiences loneliness at a boarding school. But Jane is resilient in the face of isolation, becoming strong and independent.
