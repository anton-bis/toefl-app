### Listen to a Talk in a marine biology class – Questions 25-28

audio: listening-talk1.m4a
image: 19.png

Professor: Let's talk a little about sea otters and their importance to their own and indeed other ecosystems. We'll start with a few basic facts. The sea otter is a marine mammal often found along the coastal waters of the northern Pacific. It lacks the blubber of other marine mammals, like whales or seals. But it stays warm in icy waters thanks to its fur, which is the densest of any animal's. It also has unusually large lungs, two and a half times larger than those of similarly sized land mammals, and this helps it stay buoyant and dive efficiently. Sea otters have a loose flap of skin under each forearm that functions like a pocket, which they use for storing food and tools. That's right, tools. Sea otters are one of the few animals that use tools, using clam shells for digging and rocks to open clam shells. Now, wonderful as they may be, why care about sea otters? Because sea otters eat sea urchins, and sea urchins eat kelp. Kelp is a large seaweed that can grow up to thirty meters tall, providing food and shelter for a range of marine life. Kelp forests support a greater variety of marine life than almost any other ocean habitat. Kelp also grows very densely and acts as a natural buffer, protecting shorelines from storm damage. Finally, kelp forests are like rain forests in that they absorb and store carbon dioxide through photosynthesis, mitigating climate change and reducing ocean acidification.

audio: listening-talk2.m4a
image: 20.png

Professor: So today we're looking at the hero's journey, a storytelling pattern that shows up in myths, literature, and narratives from all over the world. This structure was brought into the spotlight by Joseph Campbell, an American mythologist. In his book, The Hero with a Thousand Faces, Campbell studied myths across cultures and noticed they shared a common structure. Campbell called it the monomyth, and he believed it reflected something universal about the human experience. The journey usually kicks off with a call to adventure, some kind of invitation or challenge that pulls the hero out of their everyday life. They might resist at first, but eventually they accept, often with a mentor's help. Then come the trials, tests that push the hero to grow. At the center of it all is the abyss, the toughest part, where the hero faces their greatest fear or enemy. If they make it through, they're transformed. And here's the key part. They return with a gift, some kind of insight, wisdom, or ability that can help others. That gift is what makes the journey meaningful, not just for the hero, but for their community. Now, some critics argue this model oversimplifies storytelling. It can flatten out cultural differences and force diverse stories into a single mold. Still, I'd argue it's influential because it taps into something deeply human: the idea that struggle leads to growth, and that we come back changed.

### Listen to a Talk in a business class – Questions 8-11

audio: listening-m2talk1.m4a
image: 26.png

Professor: Let's talk about lean manufacturing and its impact on efficiency in production processes. Lean manufacturing is a systematic approach to minimizing waste within a manufacturing system while maximizing productivity. While the methodology has been adopted by many companies worldwide, it was developed by a Japanese carmaker in the mid twentieth century. But its roots can actually be traced to the supermarket. In the nineteen fifties, Japanese engineers observed that supermarkets in the United States only restocked items as customers purchased them, maintaining just enough inventory to meet demand. This idea of just in time inventory management became a core principle of lean manufacturing. By identifying and removing excess inventory and eliminating other inefficiencies, companies could reduce costs and increase their overall productivity. Another important aspect of lean manufacturing is continuous improvement, often referred to as kaizen. This involves constantly seeking ways to enhance processes, products, and services. Employees at all levels are encouraged to contribute ideas for improvement, fostering a culture of innovation and teamwork. Lean manufacturing also emphasizes the importance of value from the customer's perspective. Companies focus on producing goods that meet customer needs and expectations, ensuring that each step in the production process adds value rather than unnecessary complexity. As you might imagine, implementing lean manufacturing requires a shift in organizational culture. Resistance to change and difficulty in sustaining improvements can get in the way. That said, companies that successfully adopt lean practices experience increased efficiency, reduced costs, and higher customer satisfaction.

### Listen to a Talk in a music appreciation class – Questions 12-15

audio: listening-m2talk2.m4a
image: 27.png

Professor: Most wind instruments, which are played by blowing in, went through lots of modifications before becoming the standardized instruments we see and hear today. Now, most brass instruments, like the trumpet and the trombone, underwent many centuries of refinement before being standardized. But the tuba only took about 180 years to reach its current form. That's because, through the centuries, composers and inventors wanted an ideal bass instrument to play those really low notes. Rather than tinker with an instrument they didn't love, they discarded it and started again. So the tuba doesn't really have a clear ancestor, more a series of unrelated precursors trying to do what it now does. An early example was the serpent. The serpent was made of a wooden tube, bent into the shape of, well, a serpent, the letter S. Problem was, it didn't sound great. The wooden tube was too wide, which caused problems with intonation, meaning it was hard to hit the right notes. Imagine a choir of voices accompanied by a serpent, booming along out of tune. Ouch. Well, so much for the serpent. And hello, ophicleide. The ophicleide's main advantage was that it was made of brass. The brass construction made its intonation much better. The ophicleide became much more popular than the serpent. The composer Berlioz used it in some famous pieces, but still, the ophicleide wasn't the answer. Its range was pretty narrow, and composers wanted something that could play lower notes.
