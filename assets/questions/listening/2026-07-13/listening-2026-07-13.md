Professor: Imagine a time when the only lights available were flickering candles or oil lamps. Now fast forward to today, when life is illuminated by the glow of energy-efficient LED lights. So what makes these tiny light-emitting diodes so revolutionary? The first practical visible-spectrum LED was developed in 1962. It was a breakthrough that altered the landscape of lighting technology and set us on a path toward reduced energy consumption. LEDs, short for light-emitting diodes, are a type of solid-state lighting. They use a semiconductor to convert electricity directly into light. Unlike traditional incandescent bulbs, which waste a lot of energy as heat, LEDs are incredibly efficient. Their biggest benefit is that they use about seventy-five percent less energy than incandescent bulbs and last twenty-five times longer. They have enabled new designs that were previously unimaginable because of their small size, allowing LEDs to be integrated into flexible strips, smart bulbs, and even clothing. They also create possibilities in architecture, art, and everyday consumer products. With ongoing research, we could see LEDs that purify the air, improve mood through human-centric lighting, or even transform communication through Li-Fi, a wireless system that uses light from LEDs.

### Listen to a Talk in a Psychology Class - Questions 29-32

subtitle: Lecture: Psychology Reinforcement
audio: listening_m1_q29_q32_lecture_psychology_reinforcement.mp3
image: 20.png

Professor: Positive reinforcement is a powerful concept in psychology and behavior training. It means giving a reward after someone performs an action you want them to do again. The basic idea is that when a behavior leads to a positive outcome, it is more likely to be repeated. This principle applies to both humans and animals, but the way it works can differ. Animals need to receive rewards immediately after the desired behavior. For example, if a dog sits and gets a treat right away, it learns that sitting leads to something good. If the reward comes too late, the connection is lost. Humans, however, can understand delayed rewards. They may work hard today because they are aiming for a future goal, like earning a degree, or they may feel motivated by praise even if the reward is not tangible or instant. But rewards must be used carefully. If a parent gives a child candy to stop whining, the child may learn that whining gets rewarded. It is like watering weeds instead of flowers; you are encouraging the wrong behavior. If rewards are given too frequently, they can reduce a person's internal motivation, making the person rely more on external incentives. So while positive reinforcement is effective, it works best when applied thoughtfully and with clear intent.

audio: listening_m2_q08_q11_lecture_marketing_viral.mp3
image: 26.png

Professor: Today, we are diving into the dynamic and often unpredictable world of viral marketing. Viral marketing is a strategy that has redefined how brands connect with audiences in the digital age. It harnesses the power of social networks, relying on individuals to share content that resonates, entertains, or provokes thought. When executed effectively, a viral campaign can generate massive exposure and amplify visibility at a fraction of the cost of traditional advertising. What makes viral marketing striking is its potential for exponential reach. A single post, video, or meme can cascade through online communities, crossing geographic and demographic boundaries in mere hours. But this strategy is far from foolproof. One of the biggest challenges is unpredictability. There is no guaranteed formula for what will go viral; what resonates with one audience may be ignored by another. Content that is edgy or controversial may be more likely to go viral, but it can also spark backlash and damage a company's reputation. Another risk is loss of control. Once content goes viral, it is no longer in the hands of the brand. Memes, remixes, and commentary can distort the original message in ways that conflict with the brand's values or goals. Despite these risks, viral marketing remains an invaluable tool, particularly for startups and emerging brands seeking rapid traction. Next, let's talk about case studies of effective viral marketing campaigns.
