Professor: Before we say any more about textile production, let's talk about the historical indigo trade, indigos, a natural blue dye coming from the leaves of Indigofera plants. Today you see it most often in blue jeans. Indigo was a staple export for hundreds of years. There was a time when only rice was more valuable. The English began importing it during the early 1600's from India, and British colonists started growing it in North America about a century later. Natural indigo nearly vanished from industrial use by the early twentieth century after German chemists invented a synthetic indigo. Farmers find it to be low maintenance with high returns: one hectare can yield thousands of kilograms of leaves. The economic incentive is clear: minimal input, high output, and growing international interest. That said, the traditional fermentation techniques for extracting the dye are extremely labor intensive, which makes natural indigo expensive, and some producers claiming natural methods still rely on harsh chemicals which undermines the dye's eco-friendly reputation.

### Listen to a Talk in a Psychology Class – Questions 25-28

subtitle: Delayed Rewards and Positive Reinforcement
audio: L07_Delayed_Rewards_and_Positive_Reinforcement.mp3

Professor: Positive reinforcement is a powerful concept in psychology and behavior training. It means giving a reward after someone performs an action you want them to do again. The basic idea is that when a behavior leads to a positive outcome, it's more likely to be repeated. In other words, we tend to repeat actions that bring us something positive. This principle applies to both humans and animals, but the way it works can differ. Animals need to receive rewards immediately after the desired behavior. For example, if a dog sits and gets a treat right away, it learns that sitting leads to something good. If the reward comes too late, the connection is lost. Humans, however, can understand delayed rewards. They might work hard today because they're aiming for a future goal, like earning a degree. Or they might feel motivated by praise even if the reward isn't tangible or instant. But rewards must be used carefully. If a parent gives a child candy to stop whining, the child may learn that whining gets rewarded. It's like watering weeds instead of flowers. You're encouraging the wrong behavior. And if rewards are given too frequently, they can reduce a person's internal motivation, making them rely more on external incentives to take action. So while positive reinforcement is effective, it works best when applied thoughtfully and with clear intent.

### Listen to a Talk in a Physics Class – Questions 41-44

subtitle: Soap Bubbles and Thin Films
audio: L11_Soap_Bubbles_and_Thin_Films.mp3

Professor: Today we're going to explore a phenomenon called thin film interference. This is an optical effect that occurs when light interacts with very thin surfaces. Imagine a soap bubble floating in the air. Although it's made of clear liquid, it shines with shifting rainbow colors. Thin film interference explains how this shimmering display occurs. When light strikes a bubble, some reflects off the outer surface and some reflects off the inner surface. As these waves meet, they can either work together or work against each other. If they reinforce one another, the color looks brighter. This is called constructive interference because the waves are constructing or building the light. If the waves hit each other and cancel each other out, the color fades. This is destructive interference because the waves are destroying each other's effect. Which color appears depends on factors that keep changing. Factors like the thickness of the film and the angle of the light. As these factors shift, some wavelengths are reinforced while others are canceled. And that constant change is what produces the bubble's rainbow shimmer. The thin film that creates these dazzling colors also makes the bubble fragile. Because the layer is so delicate, the bubble can burst with the slightest touch or breeze. Scientists are interested in applying thin film interference to technology. Possible uses include paints that shine with bright colors without pigments and anti-reflective coatings for lenses. Yet reproducing the bubble's natural balance of simplicity and efficiency remains a significant challenge.

Professor: The Eastgate Center in Harare, Zimbabwe is a mixed use complex combining retail spaces and offices. Energy costs were high, and importing advanced air conditioners was impossible. To solve this dilemma, Pearce looked to termite mounds. Termites are insects that live in large colonies. They build big mounds on the ground throughout the African savannah. The mounds are amazing structures. Some rise 3 meters aboveground and extend another one to 3 meters below the ground. The underground chambers connect to the above-ground chimneys through a maze of tunnels, and this entire system works like natural climate control. Warm air rises and escapes through the top, while cooler air flows in from below. The thick walls of soil and clay absorb heat during the day, and release it at night. Termites fine tune air flow by opening and closing vents, adding or removing soil to enlarge or seal passages. On hot days, they open more vents for cooling. At night, they close them to keep warmth in. Pearce borrowed these ideas. The Eastgate Center's walls are made from thick materials like concrete and brick, and there's a system of ducts and adjustable louvers throughout the building to mimic the vents. The result? A building that uses far less energy than its conventional counterparts.

### Listen to a Talk in a Biology Class – Questions 53-56

subtitle: Keystone Species and Ecosystem Recovery
audio: L14_Keystone_Species_and_Ecosystem_Recovery.mp3

Professor: In the early 1900s, sea otters, marine mammals that feed on invertebrates including sea urchins, were nearly wiped out along the United States Pacific coast by fur hunters. With otters gone, urchin numbers exploded, destroying kelp forests. Kelp is a type of seaweed, and it's sea urchins' favorite food. This left countless marine species without the protective habitat that kelp forests provided to them. In 1972, otters were a keystone species, organisms whose impact on ecosystems is far greater than their numbers suggest. A similar case occurred in Yellowstone National Park. Wolves, the park's top predator, had been eradicated. Populations of elk, a deer-like animal and a favorite prey for wolves, soared. Their huge populations damaged local vegetation, including the willow and aspen trees for which the park had been famous. In the 1990s, wolves were reintroduced to the park to get the elk population under control. But the wolves didn't just cut elk numbers; they changed elk behavior. Afraid of being eaten by wolves, the elk avoided stream-side areas where they're especially vulnerable. This helped willow and aspen trees reestablish themselves there. The trees brought back beavers, animals that live in streams and build dams to create ponds for themselves. The beaver dams created habitats for fish, birds, and amphibians, a huge cascading effect because of this other keystone species.

Professor: Imagine taking a regular piano and transforming its sound by inserting everyday objects like screws, bolts, and rubber erasers between the strings. This is what's known as a prepared piano. It was developed by the avant garde composer John Cage in 1940. Cage was working at a university in Seattle, and he needed to write music with percussion to accompany dancers, but the performance space's stage wasn't big enough for percussion instruments like drums, so he made the sounds with just the piano by placing objects between the strings. Now, Cage spent time searching for items that made the kind of sounds he was looking for. It had a feel of improvisation. Cage himself said that looking for items was kind of like walking on the beach and looking for shiny or beautiful seashells that you liked. That is, he didn't know ahead of time what items he wanted to use. He just kept looking around and seeing what suited his needs. Cage ended up composing a collection of music for prepared piano in the mid-1940s. He included specific instructions for how to prepare the piano. For this collection, Cage was inspired by ancient Hindu theory and about eight essential emotions. He wanted his listeners to sense eight essential emotions from Hindu philosophy. The result is a blending of sounds that would be familiar to a western audience, plus sounds reminiscent of Indonesian gamelan. Um, gamelan is traditional Indonesian percussion music. This prepared piano music really raised John Cage's profile in the music world.

### Listen to a Talk on an Economics Podcast – Questions 61-64

subtitle: Behavioral Economics and Cognitive Biases
audio: L16_Behavioral_Economics_and_Cognitive_Biases.mp3

Professor: Today, we're discussing behavioral economics, a fascinating field that explores how psychological factors influence economic decision making. Traditionally, economists assumed that individuals made rational choices. However, behavioral economics challenges this notion by demonstrating that people often behave irrationally because of cognitive biases and emotions. One classic example is the concept of loss aversion. Studies show that individuals tend to prefer avoiding losses rather than acquiring equivalent gains. For instance, losing 100 euros feels worse than gaining 100 euros feels good. This bias can lead to risk-averse behavior. This bias can lead to risk-averse behavior impacting consumer decisions. The risk of loss seems intolerable even when there is an equal or greater opportunity for gain. Another factor is present bias, where individuals disproportionately value immediate rewards over future benefits. This can make it hard to save for retirement and can lead people to overspend. Another behavior that economists examine is confirmation. This is a form of confirmation bias, where individuals favor information that confirms their pre existing beliefs while disregarding contradictory data. Imagine a consumer who strongly believes that Brand X makes the best smartphones. Because of the confirmation bias, they might only seek out favorable reviews about Brand X while ignoring negative ones. Even when presented with objective comparisons showing that another brand has better performance, features, or is more reliable, they will downplay that information.
