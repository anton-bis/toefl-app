### Task 7 Read an Academic Passage — Radio Astronomy: A New Perspective (Questions 58–62)

Radio astronomy is the study of celestial objects using radio waves rather than visible light. Unlike optical telescopes, radio telescopes can detect signals from sources obscured by dust or too faint to be seen with traditional instruments. This branch of astronomy has led to transformative discoveries, including the detection of cosmic microwave background radiation—a faint glow left over from the Big Bang—which has enabled scientists to reconstruct the universe's early conditions.

One of the most famous radio astronomy discoveries was the identification of pulsars in 1967. These rapidly spinning neutron stars, the dense remnants of massive stars that have exploded, emit beams of radio waves at regular intervals. Observing pulsars helps scientists understand the end stages of stellar life and the behavior of matter under extreme conditions.

However, radio astronomy faces growing challenges due to technological interference. Devices like mobile phones, wireless routers, and satellites emit radio frequencies that can overwhelm the faint cosmic signals astronomers seek to detect. This "radio frequency pollution" makes it increasingly difficult to isolate genuine astronomical data. To combat this, observatories are often located in remote areas and protected by radio quiet zones, but the global spread of wireless technology continues to pose a serious threat.

### Task 9 Read an Academic Passage — Value Theory (Questions 68–72)

Value theory investigates the nature of values and the principles that determine what is worth pursuing, both individually and collectively. Central questions include: What makes something good? Are values objective features of the world or subjective preferences? A key distinction in value theory is between intrinsic value, which denotes value something has in itself, and instrumental value, which refers to the value of something as a means to an end.

One influential theory within this domain is utilitarianism, which posits that the moral worth of actions is determined by their consequences, specifically the maximization of overall happiness or utility. This consequentialist framework has shaped ethical reasoning in fields ranging from public policy to economics. However, critics argue that utilitarianism's aggregation of happiness can negate individual rights and seemingly justify morally troubling trade-offs. For example, allocating limited healthcare resources to benefit the majority may disadvantage those with rare or costly conditions, raising concerns about fairness and equity.

Contemporary value theorists often endorse value pluralism, the view that multiple, sometimes conflicting values—such as autonomy, justice, and compassion—are irreducibly important. This pluralistic approach emphasizes the complexity of ethical decision-making and the need to navigate competing goods instead of reducing them to a single metric.
