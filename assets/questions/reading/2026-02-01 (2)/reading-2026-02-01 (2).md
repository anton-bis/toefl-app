### Task 7 Read an Academic Passage – The Mysteries of Opal (Questions 31–35)

Opal is a unique gemstone, known for its vibrant colors that shift as the stone moves. Unlike most gemstones, which form rigid, repeating crystalline structures, opals are amorphous, meaning they lack a fixed internal shape. This is due to the formation process, where silica-rich water seeps into cracks in rocks deep underground, leaving silica deposits when it evaporates. Tiny silica spheres create the internal structure of opals, and these spheres diffract light, separating it into colors, much like a prism does. The color and brilliance of opals depends on the size and spacing of the silica spheres. Larger spheres produce reds and oranges, while smaller spheres result in blues and greens. Because of Australia's unique geological conditions, it is home to over 90 percent of the world's opals. Opals are also fragile. Their high water content and noncrystalline structure make them prone to cracking when exposed to sudden temperature changes. Modern jewelers use techniques such as laser cutting and humidity-controlled storage to better protect opals. Ancient cultures believed opals had mystical properties and brought good luck. The Romans saw them as a symbol of hope and purity, while the Greeks thought they gave the gift of prophecy.

### Task 2 Read an Academic Passage – The Shifting Art of Playwriting (Questions 11–15)

Innovative playwriting techniques have transformed the theatrical landscape by challenging linear storytelling and passive spectatorship. Nonlinear narratives, seen in plays like Harold Pinter's Betrayal, disrupt chronological order to reflect the fragmented nature of memory and emotion. This structure invites audiences to actively reconstruct the story, deepening their engagement.

Another innovation is the so-called breaking of the fourth wall, where characters directly address the audience. Used by Bertolt Brecht in Mother Courage and Her Children, this technique encourages viewers to reflect critically rather than become absorbed in illusion. Fully interactive theater, exemplified by the British production Sleep No More, goes further by allowing viewers to physically move through the performance space, personalizing the experience and making each viewing unique.

These techniques suggest a broader hypothesis: that theatrical form is not merely a container for content but a vital part of creating meaning. By reshaping narrative structure and audience dynamics, playwrights can evoke new emotional and intellectual responses, revealing complex aspects of the human condition.

Of course, such innovations may confuse rather than enlighten more traditional-minded theater audiences and also disrupt their emotional immersion, and fragment the narrative. Thus, while these techniques can serve as powerful tools, their success depends on thoughtful execution and a clear artistic purpose.
