### Task 4 Read an Academic Passage – The Political Legacy of the Roman Empire (Questions 31–35)

The political legacy of the Roman Empire has shaped later governments in Europe and beyond. Roman ideas about citizenship, written law, public administration, and representative institutions continued to influence societies long after the empire declined.

One of Rome's most important contributions was the idea that laws should be written, publicly known, and applied to citizens through recognizable procedures. Later legal systems adapted this principle to create codes, courts, and civic rights. Roman administration also left a model for organizing large territories through roads, provincial officials, taxation, and communication networks.

However, the Roman legacy was not copied without change. Later societies selected the parts that served their own needs. Some emphasized republican traditions and civic duty, while others drew on imperial symbols to justify centralized authority. For that reason, Rome's political influence is best understood as a flexible set of ideas rather than a single fixed system.

### Task 5 Read an Academic Passage – Coastal Erosion Challenges (Questions 36–40)

Coastal erosion poses a significant threat to communities, ecosystems, and infrastructure along shorelines. Rising sea levels, stronger storm surges, and human activities such as construction and deforestation can make vulnerable areas even more exposed. Engineers and environmental scientists therefore look for innovative solutions that can protect the land while preserving natural coastal systems.

One popular method is beach nourishment, which involves adding sand to eroding beaches. This approach helps beaches absorb wave energy, but it can be expensive and must be repeated regularly. Other structural interventions include seawalls and groynes. Seawalls are constructed parallel to the shore to protect the land from wave impact, while groynes are built perpendicular to the shoreline to trap sand and slow erosion.

Apart from structural interventions, ecological solutions are also increasingly important. Wetlands, dunes, and mangrove forests can stabilize the coast and absorb wave energy while supporting wildlife. These natural barriers can also contribute to climate resilience by reducing the impact of sea-level rise and storm surges.

### Task 6 Read an Academic Passage – Understanding Ecological Systems Theory (Questions 41–45)

Ecological Systems Theory, introduced by Urie Bronfenbrenner, revolutionized our perception of human psychological development. [A] It posits that individuals are shaped by interactions among multiple overlapping environmental systems. [B] This theory reshaped developmental research, offering a multifaceted lens that surpasses earlier, linear models. [C] By considering the dynamic interplay between a person and their environment, it highlights the multifactorial nature of psychological growth. [D]

The theory delineates several environmental layers, beginning with the microsystem, which refers to the institutions and groups that most directly impact the child's development, such as family and school. The mesosystem encompasses the relationships among the microsystems, such as the impact of a teacher's communication with parents on a child's education. Beyond these, the exosystem consists of indirect influences like a parent's workplace stress subtly affecting the home environment. The macrosystem, encompassing broader cultural and societal contexts, frames these interactions with underlying norms and policies.

While Bronfenbrenner's model offers an intricate framework, it is not without critique. Some argue that the model underestimates the role of technology, which has created virtual microsystems that transcend geographical limits. Despite these critiques, the theory remains influential in fields ranging from education to public policy, prompting continuous exploration of its applications and adaptations.

### Task 7 Read an Academic Passage – Carthage's Trade Network (Questions 46–50)

Carthage, founded by Phoenician merchants around 814 B.C.E., became a powerful city-state due to its strategic location on the Mediterranean. This position allowed Carthage to establish a vast trade network that extended across the Mediterranean and into Africa, Europe, and Asia.

Carthaginian traders dealt in goods such as silver, gold, tin, and textiles. The Carthaginians excelled in maritime technology, constructing advanced ships for long-distance journeys and bulky cargo transport. They developed efficient trade routes and set up colonies, expanding their economic reach. Their prowess in navigation and trade transformed Carthage into one of the wealthiest cities in the ancient world.

Carthage's wealth was also built on innovations in agriculture. Carthaginian farmers employed advanced irrigation techniques and crop rotation, leading to abundant harvests that supported both local consumption and export.

However, Carthage's rise did not come without challenges. Its growing wealth and dominance in trade led to rivalry with Rome. The Punic Wars, a series of three wars fought against Rome from 264 to 146 B.C.E., were rooted in competition for control of Mediterranean trade routes. Despite initial successes, Carthage was ultimately defeated. The final blow came when the Romans launched a brutal siege on Carthage, culminating in the city's destruction.
