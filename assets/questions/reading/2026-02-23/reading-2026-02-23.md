### Task 5 Read an Academic Passage – Value Theory (Questions 26–30)

Value theory investigates the nature of values and the principles that determine what is worth pursuing, both individually and collectively. Central questions include: What makes something good? Are values objective features of the world or subjective preferences? A key distinction in value theory is between intrinsic value, which denotes value something has in itself, and instrumental value, which refers to the value of something as a means to an end.

One influential theory within this domain is utilitarianism, which posits that the moral worth of actions is determined by their consequences, specifically the maximization of overall happiness or utility. This consequentialist framework has shaped ethical reasoning in fields ranging from public policy to economics. However, critics argue that utilitarianism's aggregation of happiness can negate individual rights and seemingly justify morally troubling trade-offs. For example, allocating limited healthcare resources to benefit the majority may disadvantage those with rare or costly conditions, raising concerns about fairness and equity.

Contemporary value theorists often endorse value pluralism, the view that multiple, sometimes conflicting values—such as autonomy, justice, and compassion—are irreducibly important. This pluralistic approach emphasizes the complexity of ethical decision-making and the need to navigate competing goods instead of reducing them to a single metric.

### Task 6 Read an Academic Passage – Synthetic Diamonds (Questions 31–35)

Traditionally, diamonds were extracted from mines, a process fraught with environmental concerns. Synthetic diamonds offer a promising alternative.

Creating synthetic diamonds involves replicating the conditions under which natural diamonds form. One popular method is High Pressure High Temperature (HPHT), where carbon is subjected to extreme heat and pressure. HPHT diamonds can be produced quickly and in large quantities, making them suitable for mass production. However, they may contain metallic inclusions from the catalysts used, which can affect clarity and color.

Another method is Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD), which grows diamonds layer by layer from carbon-rich gases in a vacuum chamber. CVD diamonds typically have fewer impurities and offer greater control over clarity and size. Although CVD diamonds can be produced on a large scale, CVD production is often more technically demanding and slower, which may influence cost.

Both HPHT and CVD methods produce diamonds that are chemically and physically identical to natural diamonds. They share the same hardness, brilliance, and clarity, making them suitable for jewelry. Consumers are often skeptical of synthetic diamonds, as they associate rarity and historical significance only with natural diamonds. Nevertheless, the market for synthetic diamonds continues to grow, driven by their affordability, sustainability, and ethical appeal.

### Task 2 Read an Academic Passage – Coral Reef Restoration (Questions 11–15)

Coral reefs are defined as an underwater ecosystem formed by colonies of tiny marine animals called coral polyps. They are among the most diverse ecosystems on Earth; however, they are facing severe threats from climate change, pollution, and overfishing. These threats have led to widespread coral bleaching, where corals lose their vibrant colors and turn white. Bleaching weakens corals, making them more susceptible to disease and ultimately leading to their death.

Scientists and environmentalists are working on various restoration techniques to combat this decline. These efforts highlight the urgency of finding effective solutions to protect these vital ecosystems. One promising method is coral gardening, where fragments of healthy coral are grown in underwater nurseries and then transplanted onto damaged reefs. This technique has shown significant success in certain areas, helping to rebuild reef structures and promote biodiversity. Another innovative approach involves using electricity to stimulate coral growth. This method, known as Biorock™ technology, creates an electric field that encourages the deposition of minerals found in seawater, accelerating coral growth.

Despite these efforts, challenges remain. Restoration projects are often expensive and labor-intensive, and the long-term success of these techniques is still uncertain. Continuous research and adaptive management strategies are crucial for ensuring the resilience of coral reefs in the face of ongoing environmental changes.
