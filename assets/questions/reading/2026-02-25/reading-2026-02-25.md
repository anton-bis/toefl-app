### Task 7 Read an Academic Passage – Parallel Algorithms: From Challenges to Opportunities (Questions 31–35)

In computer science, parallel algorithms are designed to solve problems by dividing them into smaller tasks that can be processed simultaneously across multiple processors. This approach contrasts with sequential algorithms, which process tasks one at a time. Parallel algorithms are essential for handling large-scale computations, such as simulating weather patterns, analyzing genomic data, or training machine learning models.

The main advantage of parallel algorithms is speed. By distributing workloads across multiple processors, tasks that would take hours or days on a single processor can be completed in minutes. For example, in healthcare, parallel algorithms can simulate the spread of a virus across millions of data points, enabling researchers to predict outbreaks and allocate resources more effectively.

However, parallel algorithms also present challenges. One issue is load balancing—ensuring that all processors receive an equal share of work. If some processors finish their tasks early while others are still busy, the system becomes inefficient. Another challenge is communication overhead. When processors need to exchange data, the time spent transferring information can slow down the overall computation. Additionally, debugging parallel algorithms is more complex because errors may arise from interactions between processors rather than from a single faulty component.

Despite these challenges, parallel algorithms continue to evolve. Advances in hardware, such as graphics processing units (GPUs) and quantum computers, are enabling new applications. Researchers are also developing frameworks that simplify the design of parallel algorithms, making them accessible to a broader range of programmers.

### Task 2 Read an Academic Passage – Generational Identity (Questions 11–15)

Generational identity refers to the shared experiences and cultural markers that define a group of people born during a particular time period. Sociologists often categorize generations based on birth years, such as Baby Boomers (1946–1964), Generation X (1965–1980), Millennials (1981–1996), and Generation Z (1997–2012). These labels help researchers analyze how historical events, technological advancements, and social changes shape the values and behaviors of different age groups.

However, generational identity is not without its limitations. Critics argue that broad labels can oversimplify the diversity within a generation. For example, a Millennial born in 1981 may have vastly different experiences from one born in 1996, even though both fall under the same generational label. Additionally, socioeconomic factors, geographic location, and individual circumstances can create significant variation within a generation.

Despite these criticisms, generational identity remains a useful framework for understanding societal trends. It provides a lens through which researchers can examine how collective experiences—such as economic recessions, wars, or technological revolutions—influence the attitudes and choices of large groups of people. For instance, the 2008 financial crisis had a profound impact on Millennials, many of whom entered the workforce during a period of economic instability. This shared experience has been linked to trends such as delayed homeownership, increased student debt, and a preference for urban living.
